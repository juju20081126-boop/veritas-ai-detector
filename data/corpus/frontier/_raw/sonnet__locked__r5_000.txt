Silicon is a semiconductor. To make a solar cell, manufacturers dope two layers differently. The top layer is infused with phosphorus, which adds extra electrons and makes it n-type. The volume of a cylinder is found with this formula:

**V = pi x r^2 x h**

where:

- **V** is the volume,
- **r** is the radius of the circular base,
- **h** is the height of the cylinder,
- **pi** is approximately 3.14159.

**Why it works**

A cylinder is essentially a stack of identical circles. The area of one circle is pi x r^2, so the volume is that base area multiplied by the height, which is how much "stacking" there is. This is the same principle as the volume of any prism: base area times height.

**Steps to use it**

1. Measure the radius, which is half of the diameter. If you only have the diameter, divide it by 2.
2. Square the radius by multiplying it by itself.
3. Multiply the result by pi.
4. Multiply that by the height.

**Example**

A can has a radius of 3 cm and a height of 10 cm.

V = pi x 3^2 x 10 = pi x 9 x 10 = 90 x pi, which is about **282.7 cubic centimeters**.

**Tips**

- Keep your units consistent. If the radius is in centimeters and the height is in meters, convert one of them first.
- The result is in cubic units (cm^3, m^3, and so on). For liquids, 1 cm^3 equals 1 milliliter, and 1,000 cm^3 equals 1 liter.
- Using the diameter d instead, the formula becomes V = pi x d^2 x h / 4.
- For a hollow cylinder such as a pipe, subtract the inner cylinder's volume from the outer one: V = pi x h x (R^2 - r^2).

Offside is one of the most discussed rules in football (soccer). Its purpose is to stop attackers from waiting near the opponent's goal for a long pass, which would make the game less skillful and less fluid.

**The basic rule**

A player is in an offside position if, at the moment a teammate plays the ball to them, any part of their head, body or legs is closer to the opponent's goal line than both the ball and the second-to-last opponent. Usually the last two opponents are the goalkeeper and one defender. Hands and arms do not count.

**Being offside is not an offence in itself**

A player in an offside position is only penalized if, in the opinion of the referee, they become actively involved in play. This means:

- interfering with play by touching the ball played by a teammate,
- interfering with an opponent, for example by blocking the goalkeeper's line of sight or challenging for the ball, or
- gaining an advantage by playing a ball that rebounds off the goalpost, crossbar or an opponent after being in an offside position.

When this happens, the referee awards an indirect free kick to the defending team where the offence took place.

**When you cannot be offside**

- In your own half of the field.
- When level with the second-to-last opponent or level with the last two opponents.
- When you receive the ball directly from a goal kick, a throw-in or a corner kick.

**Example**

Imagine a striker standing slightly ahead of the last defender when a midfielder passes the ball forward. If the striker runs onto the ball, play is stopped for offside. If the striker had been level with or behind the defender at the moment of the pass, the goal would stand. What matters is the position at the moment the ball is played, not when the striker receives it.

**VAR and assistant referees**

Assistant referees run along the touchline to monitor offside, raising a flag when they see an offence. Many professional leagues now use VAR (Video Assistant Referee) and semi-automated offside technology to judge tight calls, tracking player positions with cameras and sensors.

**The offside trap**

Defenders use the rule tactically. By stepping forward together as a line just before a pass is played, they can leave attackers in an offside position. It takes good coordination and risks leaving the goal exposed if a single player mistimes the move.

The proverb "All work and no play makes Jack a dull boy" is a warning that someone who does nothing but work will become boring, tired and eventually less effective. It has been in English for centuries, with versions appearing in collections of proverbs as early as the 1600s.

**The idea behind it**

Play and rest are not the opposite of productivity. They are part of what makes it possible. A person who works constantly gets worn down. Concentration slips, creativity dries up, and stress piles up. Time spent on hobbies, friends and relaxation refreshes the mind, so the work that gets done afterward is usually better.

**Why "dull"?**

In this saying, "dull" works in two senses. Jack becomes dull in the sense of being uninteresting, with nothing to talk about except work. He is also dull in the sense of a blunt blade, a mind that has lost its sharpness. Both meanings suggest that constant effort without variety wears a person down.

**What research suggests**

Modern psychology supports the proverb. Studies on burnout show that people who rarely take breaks have lower motivation and more mistakes. Rest allows the brain to consolidate memories and make new connections, which is why good ideas often arrive in the shower or on a walk. Play also supports mental health, social bonds and problem-solving.

**A cultural echo**

The phrase is famously used in Stephen King's novel *The Shining* and Stanley Kubrick's film version, where Jack Torrance types the line over and over on page after page. It becomes a chilling symbol of isolation and obsession, which is the very thing the proverb warns about.

**Takeaway**

Balance is the point. Hard work matters, but a life without enjoyment is poorer and less effective. Make time to rest and play, and you will usually work better too.
