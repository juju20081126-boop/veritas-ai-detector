That vertical strip is a **security thread**, and it is one of the main features that makes a $20 bill hard to counterfeit.

**What it is**

The thread is a thin polymer strip set into the paper when the note is made. It is not printed on the surface. It sits inside the cotton-linen blend that US currency is printed on, so you can barely see it in normal light. Hold the bill up to a lamp or a window and it shows up as a dark line running from top to bottom.

On a $20, the thread is to the left of Andrew Jackson's portrait. If you look closely you'll see tiny printing on it that reads "USA TWENTY," along with a small flag. The text alternates direction, so you can read it from either side of the note.

**Why it's there**

Counterfeiters usually work with ordinary paper and a good printer or copier. A scanner can reproduce what's printed on the surface of a bill, but it can't reproduce something embedded inside the paper. A fake might have a line drawn or printed where the thread should be, but it will be visible without backlighting, it won't carry clear microprinting, and it won't react the way the real one does under ultraviolet light.

**The UV test**

Under a UV (black) light, the security thread on a genuine $20 glows green. Each denomination glows a different color: blue for the $5, orange for the $10, yellow for the $50, and pink for the $100. The placement also changes from bill to bill, which stops people from bleaching a $5 and reprinting it as a $100. The thread position and glow color would give it away.

**How it fits with other features**

The thread is designed to be checked alongside the other security features on the note:

- A **watermark** of Jackson, also visible when held to light, on the right side
- **Color-shifting ink** in the lower-right "20," which changes from copper to green when tilted
- **Microprinting** around the portrait and borders
- Raised, slightly rough-feeling ink from intaglio printing

Security threads were added to US paper money in the early 1990s, when high-quality color copiers became cheap enough to be a real threat. Since then they've become one of the quickest checks a cashier can make: hold the bill up to the light and look for the strip.

# What Is the Best Skill in Judo?

There's no single technique that every judoka would agree is "the best," because effectiveness depends on body type, style, and ruleset. That said, if the question is which *skill* matters most, most coaches would point to one answer: **kuzushi**, the art of breaking your opponent's balance. If the question is which *throw* is most effective, a few techniques consistently stand out. Both angles are worth covering.

## Kuzushi: The Foundational Skill

Jigoro Kano, who founded judo in 1882, built the entire system around a principle often summarised as "maximum efficiency, minimum effort." The idea is that a smaller person can throw a larger one, but only if the larger person is first put off balance. A throw attempted against a stable, well-planted opponent usually fails, no matter how strong the thrower is.

Kano described every throw as having three phases:

1. **Kuzushi** – unbalancing the opponent
2. **Tsukuri** – fitting into position for the throw
3. **Kake** – executing the throw

Of these, kuzushi is what separates good judo from mere wrestling. Kano identified eight directions in which a person's balance can be broken (front, back, both sides, and the four diagonals). Pulling, pushing, and using your opponent's own movement to tip them onto their toes or heels are all forms of kuzushi. Without it, techniques become a contest of strength. With it, even a modest throw can land cleanly for ippon.

This is why kuzushi is arguably the "best skill": it's the ingredient that makes every other technique work.

## Kumikata: Grip Fighting

In modern competitive judo, a close second is **kumikata**, or gripping. Elite matches are often decided before anyone attempts a throw, because whoever controls the grips controls the fight. A good grip lets you generate kuzushi and set up your attacks; a poor one leaves you defending. Breaking grips, establishing your preferred hold on the lapel and sleeve, and denying your opponent theirs are skills that take years to refine. Many coaches would argue that at high levels, gripping is the most decisive practical skill.

## The Most Effective Throws

If you are looking for specific techniques, several throws have proven themselves across decades of competition.

**Seoi nage (shoulder throw).** This is one of the most iconic throws in judo and particularly suits shorter or more compact fighters. The thrower turns in, drops their centre of gravity below the opponent's, and lifts them over the shoulder. Variations like ippon seoi nage and morote seoi nage are among the most frequently scored throws in international competition.

**Uchi mata (inner thigh throw).** Often called the "king of throws," uchi mata is favoured by taller players. The thrower sweeps their leg up between the opponent's legs while pulling them forward, launching them in a high arc. It is versatile, works in many directions, and combines well with other techniques. Statistically, it's one of the top-scoring throws at world championships and Olympic Games.

**O soto gari (major outer reap).** One of the first throws many beginners learn, and one of the most powerful. The thrower breaks the opponent's balance backward and reaps their supporting leg. Its simplicity is deceptive; done well, it is devastating.

**Harai goshi and o uchi gari** also deserve a mention, especially as parts of combinations.

## Combinations and Transitions

Another strong candidate for the "best skill" is the ability to chain techniques together, known as **renraku waza** (combination techniques) and **renzoku waza** (continuous techniques). Experienced opponents can usually defend a single attack. A feint with o uchi gari that makes the opponent step back, followed immediately by seoi nage or uchi mata, is far harder to stop. Combinations work precisely because each attack creates kuzushi for the next.

Equally valuable is skill in **ne waza** (groundwork). Many matches move to the mat after a failed throw, and a judoka who can transition smoothly into a pin (osaekomi), armlock, or choke has a major advantage.

## Conclusion

If you need a single answer for your assignment, the best skill in judo is **kuzushi**, because it underpins every successful technique and reflects Kano's core principle of efficiency. In terms of specific throws, **uchi mata** and **seoi nage** are widely regarded as the most effective, with the choice depending largely on body type. In practice, though, the best judoka are those who combine strong kuzushi, intelligent gripping, a reliable signature throw, and the ability to link techniques together, both standing and on the ground.

**1. Consistency between values and conduct**

A useful starting point is to examine whether one's actions align with one's professed principles. Many individuals hold admirable values in the abstract; moral character is more reliably demonstrated by conduct, particularly when adherence to those values is inconvenient, costly, or unobserved.

**2. Treatment of others**

How one treats people who can offer no benefit in return is widely regarded as a meaningful indicator. Courtesy toward service staff, patience with those in vulnerable positions, and fairness toward people with less power or status often reveal more about character than behaviour toward peers or superiors.

**3. Consideration of consequences**

From a consequentialist perspective, a good person seeks to reduce harm and promote the well-being of others. One may reasonably ask whether one's decisions, on balance, tend to leave others better or worse off, and whether one gives due weight to the interests of those affected.

**4. Respect for duties and rights**

Deontological traditions emphasise honesty, the keeping of promises, and respect for the autonomy and dignity of others, irrespective of outcomes. Reflecting on whether one honours commitments and refrains from using people merely as means to an end is therefore instructive.

**5. Cultivation of virtue**

Virtue ethics, originating with Aristotle, focuses less on individual acts and more on character traits such as honesty, courage, compassion, and justice. Under this view, goodness is developed through habitual practice rather than achieved once and for all.

Perhaps the most practical indicator is one's response to error. All people act wrongly at times. Goodness is best understood not as a fixed status but as an ongoing orientation: a sustained commitment to acting with integrity, fairness, and care. Seeking honest feedback from trusted individuals and engaging in regular reflection can provide a more balanced assessment than introspection alone.

The Scheveningen (pawns on e6 and d6 for Black in the Open Sicilian) is a flexible "small centre" setup, and White has several well-established ways to play against it. They fall into a few broad families.

## 1. The Keres Attack (6.g4)

After 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6, the immediate **6.g4** is one of White's most aggressive and respected tries.

- **Idea:** Kick the f6-knight with g4–g5 before Black has castled, gaining space and disrupting Black's development.
- White typically follows up with h4, Rg1, Be3, Qd2 or Qe2, and long castling.
- Black often responds with ...h6 to slow down g5, or with ...Nc6/...a6 setups, accepting a sharp middlegame.

Because of the Keres, many Black players reach the Scheveningen via the Najdorf (5...a6 then ...e6) or the Taimanov/Paulsen move orders to sidestep it.

## 2. The English Attack (Be3, f3, Qd2, g4, O-O-O)

This is the main modern approach, especially against Najdorf-Scheveningen hybrids.

- **Setup:** Be3, f3, Qd2, castle queenside, then g4–g5 and h4–h5.
- **Idea:** Opposite-side castling leads to a pawn race. White storms the kingside while Black counterattacks on the queenside with ...b5–b4, ...Rc8, and pressure down the c-file.
- f3 supports g4 and prevents ...Ng4 hitting the e3-bishop.
- Key themes include Nd5 sacrifices, g5 to dislodge the f6-knight, and timing the pawn storm to arrive first.

## 3. The Classical Approach (Be2, O-O, f4)

The older, more positional system, associated with players like Karpov.

- **Typical moves:** Be2, O-O, f4, Kh1, a4, Be3, and often Qe1–g3 or Bf3.
- **a4** is an important prophylactic move, clamping down on Black's ...b5 expansion.
- **Plans for White:**
  - **e4–e5 break:** Opening the centre while Black's king is still in the middle or the d6-pawn is weak.
  - **f4–f5:** Pressuring e6, sometimes followed by fxe6 to open the f-file or gain the d5/f5 squares.
  - **g4–g5 kingside storm:** Even with kings on the same side, White can push g4–g5 to drive away the f6-knight, supported by Rf3–h3 or Qg3 ideas.
  - **Qe1–g3 or Qe1–h4:** Rerouting the queen to the kingside to put pressure on g7 and support a mating attack.

## 4. The Sozin/Velimirovic (Bc4)

When Black has played ...Nc6, White can aim the bishop at e6 with **Bc4**.

- **Velimirovic Attack:** Bc4, Be3, Qe2, O-O-O, followed by g4–g5 and sacrificial ideas like Nd5 or Nf5.
- The bishop on c4 (often retreating to b3) eyes e6 and supports sacrificial breakthroughs against Black's king.

## Recurring Themes Across All Systems

- **The d5 square:** Black often tries to keep White out of d5; White's Nd5 sacrifices are a constant tactical motif.
- **The e5 break:** When Black's pieces are passive, e4–e5 can open lines toward the king.
- **f4–f5 against e6:** Undermining the Scheveningen pawn chain.
- **Restricting ...b5:** Moves like a4 (in classical lines) slow Black's queenside play.
- **Fighting ...d5:** Black's freeing ...d6–d5 break, if achieved safely, usually equalises, so White aims to prevent or punish it.

In short, White chooses between an immediate kingside pawn storm (Keres or English Attack) and a slower buildup with f4, a4, and central or kingside breaks (Classical). The choice usually comes down to style and to the move order Black uses to reach the Scheveningen.
