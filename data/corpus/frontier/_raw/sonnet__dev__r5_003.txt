# The Roman Republic and the Roman Empire

## The Roman Republic (509-27 BCE)

**Origins.** According to tradition, Rome was founded in 753 BCE and ruled by kings. In 509 BCE the Romans expelled the last king, Tarquin the Proud, and set up a republic. Power was shared among elected magistrates (two consuls chosen each year), the Senate (an advisory body of aristocrats), and popular assemblies.

**Social conflict.** Early Republican history was shaped by the "Conflict of the Orders," in which plebeians (commoners) pushed for political rights against the patrician elite. Over about two centuries they won the right to hold office, protection through tribunes, and, in 450 BCE, a written law code, the Twelve Tables.

**Expansion.** Rome gradually conquered the Italian peninsula, binding defeated peoples to it through alliances and partial citizenship. It then fought three Punic Wars against Carthage (264-146 BCE). Hannibal's invasion of Italy nearly broke Rome, but Rome won and destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE. In the same period, Rome took control of Greece, Spain, and much of the eastern Mediterranean.

**Crisis.** Conquest brought wealth, slaves, and inequality. Small farmers lost their land, and ambitious generals gained armies loyal to themselves rather than the state. The Gracchi brothers' reform attempts (133-121 BCE) ended in violence, and the first century BCE was marked by civil wars involving Marius and Sulla, then Pompey and Julius Caesar. Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE, won the civil war, and was assassinated in 44 BCE. A further round of conflict ended when Octavian defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BCE.

## The Roman Empire (27 BCE-476 CE in the West)

**The Principate.** In 27 BCE the Senate gave Octavian the title Augustus. He kept republican forms but held real power himself, effectively becoming the first emperor. His reign began the *Pax Romana*, roughly two centuries of relative peace and prosperity across the Mediterranean world.

**Peak and growth.** Under emperors such as Trajan (r. 98-117 CE), the empire reached its greatest extent, stretching from Britain to Mesopotamia and containing perhaps 50 to 70 million people. Trade, cities, and roads flourished.

**Crisis and reform.** The third century brought civil war, plague, economic trouble, and invasions. Diocletian (r. 284-305) restored order by dividing administration between East and West. Constantine (r. 306-337) legalized Christianity, which later became the state religion, and founded Constantinople as a new capital.

**Decline.** The Western Empire faced migrations and invasions by Goths, Vandals, and others, and its last emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed in 476 CE. The Eastern Empire, usually called the Byzantine Empire, lasted until the Ottomans captured Constantinople in 1453.

## Key achievements

- **Law and government.** Roman law, from the Twelve Tables to Justinian's codification, shaped legal systems across Europe and beyond. Ideas such as written statutes, legal personhood, and the presumption of innocence have Roman roots. The Republic's mixed constitution also influenced modern political thinkers and the framers of the U.S. Constitution.
- **Engineering and architecture.** Romans built roads, aqueducts, bridges, and public baths, and they made extensive use of the arch, the vault, and concrete. The Pantheon's dome and the Colosseum still stand.
- **Military organization.** The professional legions, with their discipline, logistics, and engineering skills, were among the most effective fighting forces of the ancient world.
- **Language and literature.** Latin evolved into the Romance languages and supplied vocabulary to many others. Writers such as Cicero, Virgil, Livy, and Tacitus left works that remain central to Western literature.
- **Integration and administration.** Rome extended citizenship over time, ran provincial administration, and linked a vast territory through trade and a shared urban culture.
- **Cultural transmission.** Romans preserved and spread Greek philosophy, art, and science, and the empire's later embrace of Christianity helped shape medieval Europe.

Eleven hundred years old, the catalog said. Paper, ink, stitched binding. A thing that had survived the collapse, the Quiet Century, and two relocations of the archive itself. It smelled of dust and something faintly sweet, like old vanilla.

She had expected pictures. The people of that era were supposed to love pictures. But the pages held only words, written by hand in small, careful lines, the ink faded to the color of tea.

*I am writing this because they say the servers will not last,* the first page read. *My name is Tomas Reyes, and I was twenty-six when the war started. I want whoever finds this to know it did not begin the way they teach it.*

Ines sat down on the stool and kept reading.

Tomas wrote about the shortages, and the radio announcements that grew shorter and shorter each week. He wrote about his sister, who had walked three days to reach him. About the night the lights went out over the whole city, all at once, so that for the first time in his life he saw the stars. *Everyone went out onto the streets and looked up,* he wrote. *No one spoke. For a few minutes it was almost beautiful.*

Her textbooks had said the third world war lasted four years and ended with the Accord of Geneva. They said it was inevitable, that history had a direction. Tomas disagreed. Near the end, in a shakier hand, he wrote: *It was not fate. It was a hundred small decisions made by tired people who thought someone else would stop it.*

Ines read until the lamps dimmed for evening. Then she carefully closed the cover and sat in the quiet for a long time.

Tomorrow, she would file her report. She would describe the condition of the binding, the type of ink, the likely date.

But tonight, she wrote one more line in her own notebook, in her own clumsy hand, so someone a thousand years from now might find it too.

*Someone has to stop it.*

Viking names came from Old Norse, a language closely related to Old English, which is why many names survive in English. Place names ending in -by (Derby) and -thorpe come from Norse settlement, and surnames like Thorburn and Haldane have Norse roots. Names also had meanings: Ragnar means "counsel-army," Sigrid "victory-beautiful." English renders them differently, such as Old Norse Þórr becoming Thor, since English lacks the letters þ and ð. Translation often adjusts spelling and endings.
