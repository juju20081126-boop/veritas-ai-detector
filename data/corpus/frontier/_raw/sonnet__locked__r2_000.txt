There is no permanently dark side of the Moon. Tidal locking means the Moon always shows Earth the same face, but it still rotates once per orbit, so every part of its surface gets roughly two weeks of daylight followed by two weeks of night. The "dark side" is just the far side, the one we never see.

Meteoroids don't care about lighting, either. They strike from every direction, sunlit or not, and Earth shields almost nothing: from the Moon, our planet covers only a tiny patch of sky. So both hemispheres get hit. In fact, the far side is more heavily cratered, because lava once flooded much of the near side and covered older craters. The dark patches we see from Earth are those lava plains.

Underwater archaeology applies the methods and ethics of terrestrial archaeology to sites beneath seas, lakes, and rivers. Because submerged environments can preserve organic materials that would decay on land, shipwrecks and drowned settlements often yield exceptionally rich evidence of past societies. Investigating them, however, requires specialized techniques and technologies, which may be grouped as follows.

**Site Location and Remote Sensing**

Most projects begin with the search for a site. Researchers combine archival research, historical charts, and reports from fishers and divers with geophysical survey:

- **Side-scan sonar** produces detailed acoustic images of the seabed, revealing wreck mounds, debris fields, and other anomalies.
- **Multibeam echosounders** generate high-resolution bathymetric maps that show the form of a wreck and its relationship to the surrounding seafloor.
- **Sub-bottom profilers** penetrate sediment to detect structures that have been buried.
- **Marine magnetometers** record disturbances in the Earth's magnetic field caused by ferrous objects such as anchors, cannon, and fastenings.

In shallow coastal waters, airborne LiDAR bathymetry may also be used to map drowned landscapes and nearshore wrecks.

**Robotic and Remotely Operated Systems**

Where depth or hazardous conditions exceed the limits of human divers, remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) and autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) are employed. ROVs, which are tethered to a surface vessel, carry cameras, lighting, sonar, and manipulator arms, allowing archaeologists to inspect, sample, and recover objects in real time. AUVs follow pre-programmed survey paths and can map extensive areas with considerable precision. Such systems have made it possible to study deep-water sites, including the well-preserved ancient wrecks identified in the oxygen-free depths of the Black Sea.

**Diving Technology**

For sites within reach of divers, specialized equipment extends both safe working time and operational depth. Open-circuit SCUBA remains standard in shallow water, while surface-supplied air, mixed-gas techniques, and closed-circuit rebreathers permit longer and deeper dives. Saturation diving enables personnel to work at depth for extended periods with a single decompression at the end of the operation, and atmospheric diving suits, which maintain internal pressure at one atmosphere, have been used at sites such as the Antikythera wreck.

**Excavation and Documentation**

Underwater excavation follows the same rigorous principles as work on land. A grid or baseline is established so that every find can be located within a three-dimensional framework, and fine sediment is removed with hand fanning, airlifts, or water dredges that clear overburden without damaging fragile material. Acoustic positioning systems, together with traditional offset measurements, record the precise position of artifacts and structural elements.

Documentation has been transformed by digital methods. Photogrammetry, in which overlapping photographs are processed into accurate three-dimensional models, allows a site to be recorded in detail, measured at a later date, and shared with scholars and the public through virtual reconstructions. Laser and structured-light scanning serve a similar purpose for individual artifacts.

**Conservation and Scientific Analysis**

Objects that have lain underwater for centuries are chemically and physically unstable once recovered, and they require immediate conservation. Waterlogged wood must be kept wet and impregnated with consolidants such as polyethylene glycol before it is dried under controlled conditions, as was done with the hulls of the Vasa and the Mary Rose; smaller objects are often freeze-dried. Ceramics and metals require desalination to remove soluble salts, and iron objects encased in marine concretion are commonly examined by X-radiography or computed tomography before cleaning begins.

Scientific analysis complements this work. Dendrochronology and radiocarbon dating provide chronological frameworks, while residue analysis, isotopic studies, and DNA recovery can reveal the contents of cargoes, patterns of trade, and the origins of timber and crew.

**Legal and Ethical Considerations**

Technological capability must be matched by responsible practice. The UNESCO 2001 Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage establishes preservation in situ as the first option and prohibits the commercial exploitation of such heritage. Archaeologists therefore weigh the research value of excavation against the risk of irreversible loss, and non-invasive survey is increasingly preferred wherever possible.

**Conclusion**

Underwater archaeology depends on the integration of geophysical survey, robotics, diving technology, digital documentation, and conservation science. As these tools continue to advance, they permit ever more precise and less intrusive investigation of submerged heritage, while established ethical and legal frameworks help ensure that such discoveries remain available for future study.

Einstein is famous for two overlapping reasons: his work changed how we understand the universe, and he became a cultural icon in a way few scientists ever have.

**What he did**

- **Special relativity (1905):** He showed that space and time aren't fixed but depend on how you're moving, and that nothing can travel faster than light. A consequence of the theory is E = mc², which links mass and energy and explains why nuclear reactions release so much power.
- **Photoelectric effect (1905):** He proposed that light comes in discrete packets, now called photons, which helped launch quantum theory. This, not relativity, earned him the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics.
- **Brownian motion (1905):** His analysis of tiny particles jittering in fluid gave strong evidence that atoms really exist.
- **General relativity (1915):** A new theory of gravity in which mass and energy curve space and time. It predicted the bending of starlight, gravitational waves, and black holes, and GPS satellites need its corrections to stay accurate.

**Why he's so famous**

In 1919, astronomers measured starlight bending around the Sun during a solar eclipse, as general relativity predicted, and newspapers worldwide turned a respected physicist into a global celebrity. His ideas were strange enough to fascinate the public, and his personality helped: the wild hair, the gentle humor, and the quotable lines (many of which he never actually said).

He was also a public figure beyond physics. He left Germany as the Nazis rose to power, settled at Princeton, spoke out against racism and militarism, and signed a 1939 letter to President Roosevelt warning that atomic weapons were possible, though he never worked on the Manhattan Project. His name has since become shorthand for "genius."
