Philosophers disagree about what quantum mechanics says about reality. Here are the main interpretations.

**Copenhagen interpretation.** Associated with Bohr and Heisenberg. The wave function is a tool for predicting measurement outcomes, not a literal description of the world. Measurement "collapses" the possibilities into one result. Critics say it leaves the measurement process vague.

**Many-Worlds.** Proposed by Hugh Everett. The wave function never collapses. Every possible outcome happens, and reality branches into parallel worlds. It is mathematically simple but ontologically extravagant.

**Pilot-wave (Bohmian) mechanics.** Particles always have definite positions, guided by a real wave. It restores determinism but requires nonlocal influences.

**Objective collapse (GRW).** Collapse is a real physical process that occurs spontaneously, which makes it testable in principle.

**QBism and relational views.** The quantum state expresses an agent's beliefs or is relative to an observer, so it is not an objective property of the world.

For your assignment, you could frame these around realism versus anti-realism and determinism versus indeterminism.

# Surviving in the African Bush

Survival experts use a rule of thumb called the "rule of threes": you can last about three minutes without air, three hours in harsh exposure, three days without water, and three weeks without food. In the African bush, water and heat are the pressing problems, and the animals add a danger of their own. Here is how I would prioritize.

## 1. Stay calm and stop moving

The first mistake most people make is panicking and wandering off. Sit down, take a few slow breaths, and take stock of what you have: water, clothing, a phone, a lighter, a knife, a mirror, anything. Then think about how you got here. If people know your route and expect you back, **staying put is almost always the better choice**, because a stationary person near a vehicle or a landmark is far easier to find than a moving one. If your vehicle broke down, stay with it. A car is visible from the air, gives shade, and has useful materials such as mirrors, reflective surfaces, a battery, and oil for a smoky fire.

Only leave if you are certain no one is looking for you, you know exactly where help is, and you have enough water to reach it.

## 2. Water comes first

In the heat of the bush you can become dangerously dehydrated within a day or two. Your priorities:

- **Ration sweat, not water.** Rest in the shade through the hottest hours, roughly 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., and move only at dawn, dusk, or by moonlight if you must move.
- **Keep your clothes on.** Loose, light-colored clothing, a hat, and long sleeves reduce water loss and sun exposure.
- **Don't eat if you have no water.** Digestion uses water.
- **Look for natural sources.** Animals are guides. Birds flying low in the morning and evening toward a spot, bees, and large herds often point to water. Dry riverbeds can hold water just below the surface in the outer bends. Dig a hole there and let it seep in.
- **Collect dew.** Tie absorbent cloth around your ankles and walk through grass at dawn, then wring it out. You can also leave cloth or plastic out overnight.
- **Purify what you find.** Boil it if you can. If not, filter it through cloth. A dirty source is still better than none if the alternative is collapse, but treat it as a last resort.

Never drink urine or blood. They make dehydration worse.

## 3. Shelter and shade

Sun and heat are your main threat during the day, and nights can get surprisingly cold, especially on the high plateau and in the dry season.

- Make shade with a vehicle, a tree, a tarp, or a thick bush. Raise a cover a foot or so above your body so air can circulate.
- Insulate yourself from the hot ground. Sit or lie on a pile of grass or branches.
- At night, build up your insulation with dry grass, leaves, and layers of clothing.
- Choose your spot carefully. Avoid camping directly on game trails, at waterholes, or under trees with dead limbs. Avoid riverbeds that could flash flood if rain falls upstream.

## 4. Signal for rescue

Rescuers need to see or hear you.

- **Fire.** Keep a small fire ready to light. At night, flames show up from far away. In the daytime, add green leaves or something damp to produce thick smoke.
- **Mirror or any shiny object.** A flash can be seen for miles, and you can sweep it across the horizon or toward aircraft.
- **Ground signals.** Make large, high-contrast shapes in an open area: three of anything (fires, rocks, piles of branches) is a recognized distress sign.
- **Whistle or phone.** Three blasts repeated is the universal distress signal. Check your phone for even a weak signal, and send a text, since texts often get through when calls don't. Conserve the battery.

## 5. Deal with wildlife sensibly

Most animals want to avoid you. The real risks come from surprising them or getting between a mother and her young.

- **Lions and leopards:** Don't run, because running triggers a chase. Stand tall, face the animal, speak firmly, and back away slowly. Keep a fire going at night. Predators are generally wary of flames.
- **Elephants and buffalo:** Give them wide berth, stay downwind where possible, and avoid dense bush where you might blunder into them.
- **Hippos:** They are responsible for many deaths. Stay away from water edges at night, when they graze on land, and never get between a hippo and the water.
- **Snakes and scorpions:** Watch where you put hands and feet, shake out shoes and clothing, and don't turn over rocks without a stick.
- **Hyenas and jackals:** Mostly scavengers, but they can be bold at night near a lone person. Make noise and keep a fire.
- **Insects:** Mosquitoes and tsetse flies carry disease. Cover skin and use smoke if you can.

Keep your food, if you have any, sealed and away from where you sleep.

## 6. Food is a lower priority

Because you can survive for weeks without food, don't take risks to find it, especially if water is scarce. If you're stuck for many days, you can eat things you can positively identify. But in an unfamiliar region, **avoid unknown plants and mushrooms**, because a poisoning would cost you water and strength. Insects such as termites and grasshoppers are a safe and nutritious option when cooked. Honey from wild bees is high in calories but defended fiercely, so it is rarely worth the risk.

## 7. Staying healthy

- Watch for signs of heat illness: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, and cessation of sweating. Cool down immediately in shade, wet clothing, and rest.
- Protect your feet. Blisters and infections become serious without treatment.
- Clean any cuts with the cleanest water available and keep them covered.
- Stay mentally active. Set small tasks, such as collecting wood, checking signals, and rationing water on a schedule. Morale matters a lot, and people with a plan tend to outlast those who give up.

## If you must walk out

Only move if you have to. Travel at night or early morning, follow a drainage or a road if you can find one, and mark your route so rescuers can follow it. Leave notes at your starting point with the date, time, and direction you are heading. Pace yourself and rest often.

## The short version

Stay put, find shade, ration water, signal constantly, keep away from wildlife, and keep your head clear. Most people rescued in the bush are found because they stayed visible and stayed put, not because they marched off heroically.
