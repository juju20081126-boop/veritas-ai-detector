A helicopter autorotation is a way of landing safely after the engine fails or the tail rotor stops working. Instead of the engine driving the main rotor, the upward flow of air through the rotor, caused by the helicopter descending, keeps the blades turning. The pilot uses the stored energy in the spinning rotor to cushion the landing.

The principle is similar to a maple seed spinning as it falls. In normal flight, air is pulled down through the rotor disc. In autorotation, the helicopter descends, so air moves up through the disc and spins the blades. The rotor speed (RPM) has to be kept in a safe range, since too little means the blades lose lift and too much risks structural damage.

**Steps in a typical autorotation**

1. **Recognize the failure.** The pilot notices the warning horn or light, a change in engine noise, or a yaw of the nose as the torque disappears. Quick recognition is critical.

2. **Lower the collective immediately.** This reduces the pitch of the main rotor blades, which reduces drag and lets the rotor keep spinning. Doing this in about a second or two is vital, because the rotor will begin to slow down right away.

3. **Maintain rotor RPM.** The pilot checks the tachometer and adjusts the collective to keep RPM in the green arc.

4. **Establish the glide.** The pilot uses cyclic control to set the best glide speed, which differs by aircraft, often around 60 to 80 knots. Rotor RPM and airspeed are the two things to keep in check.

5. **Choose a landing area.** The pilot picks a suitable spot, ideally into the wind and clear of obstacles, and steers toward it. Pedals are used to keep the helicopter in trim.

6. **Make a radio call and prepare for landing.** If time allows, the pilot declares an emergency and does a quick check for the cause of the failure, such as an attempt to restart the engine.

7. **Flare.** At roughly 40 to 100 feet above the ground, the pilot pulls the cyclic back to raise the nose. This slows the forward speed and the rate of descent, and it also increases rotor RPM.

8. **Level the aircraft.** Just before touchdown, the pilot levels the helicopter with the cyclic so that it will land on its skids or wheels.

9. **Cushion the landing.** Near the ground, the pilot pulls up the collective to use the rotor's remaining energy, which slows the descent and softens the touchdown.

10. **Land and lower the collective.** After touchdown the pilot keeps the cyclic neutral, lowers the collective fully, and brings the aircraft to a stop.

Pilots practice autorotations regularly in training, often to a power recovery, so the maneuver becomes instinctive.

Critics raise reasonable concerns. School schedules are already crowded, so adding a requirement means something else must give.
