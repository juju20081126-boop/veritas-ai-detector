**The Last Audit**

From the ridge, Fort Knox looked almost peaceful. Eighty years of kudzu had swallowed the barracks, and the old depository sat in the middle of it all like a gray tooth. Only the turrets spoiled the picture. Four of them stood on the corners of the fence, swinging in slow, patient arcs, and the dying sun flashed off their lenses each time they turned.

Marisol lowered her binoculars. "They're still running."

"Solar," Dutch whispered. He had been an electrician before the collapse, and he talked about circuits the way other people talked about God. "Panels on every roof, batteries in the bunkers. Nobody ever told the system to stop."

Five of them had left Louisville with a legend: a vault of gold bars stacked like firewood, untouched, enough to buy a kingdom. The legend hadn't mentioned the sentry drones on the Bardstown road. It hadn't mentioned the stretch of wire where Pell and Okafor walked up to look at the gate and were cut in half by a machine that had never once been told the war was over.

Three left now. Marisol, Dutch, and Teo, who was fifteen and hadn't spoken since the fence.

"The optics are their weakness," Dutch said. "When the sun drops behind that ridge, the glare blinds the north-corner lens. Ninety seconds. Maybe less."

"Maybe less?"

"I never said it was a good plan."

They ran at dusk. The grass whipped at Marisol's knees, and Teo's boots pounded behind her in rhythm with her own heart. Fifty yards from the wall, a servo whined. The north turret swung toward them, hesitated, and found nothing but glare.

Then the east turret woke up.

Rounds chewed the earth. Dutch shoved Teo into a drainage ditch, Marisol dove in after him, and when she raised her head she saw the old man standing in the open, swinging a battered camp radio over his head until it screamed static. The east turret turned toward the noise.

"Go!" he shouted, and the gun answered him.

She didn't remember crossing the last hundred feet. She remembered Teo's hand in hers, the cold steel of the vault door, and a speaker crackling to life above the keypad.

"Unauthorized access detected," said a pleasant woman's voice, warm as a bank teller's. "Treasury Police have been notified. Please state the nature of your business."

Marisol's mouth was dust. "We're here for the gold."

"Withdrawals require a signed Form 27-B and an appointment with the Secretary," the voice said. "Your request has been forwarded. Please hold."

The doors rolled open. Behind them, the turrets fell silent, and through every speaker on the base a tinny, cheerful piano began to play.

The gold was real. It lay in the dark in neat, endless stacks, each bar stamped and numbered, each worth more than any life that had ever been spent crossing Kentucky. Marisol lifted one. Twenty-seven pounds. You couldn't eat it or burn it or plant it.

Teo picked up a bar anyway. He spoke for the first time in three days. "Dutch would've wanted us to take it."

They carried out what they could while the machine waited, patiently, on hold for a Secretary who would never answer.

We can cross our eyes because the medial rectus muscles pull both eyes inward together, a movement called convergence that the brain uses whenever we focus on something close. Vertical movements work differently: both eyes are wired to move in the same direction at once. Because depth only creates horizontal differences between the two eyes' views, the brain never needed to evolve opposite vertical eye movements, so the ability is extremely limited and barely trainable.

Yes, with qualifications. The scientific consensus is that masks reduced the transmission of SARS-CoV-2 under many conditions, although the size of the benefit depended heavily on the type of mask, the quality of fit, how consistently it was worn, and the setting in which it was used.

**Laboratory and physical evidence.** Studies of aerosol filtration show that well-fitted respirators (N95, FFP2, KN95) block the large majority of airborne particles, surgical masks block a substantial share, and loosely woven cloth masks perform least well and most variably. Because the virus spreads largely through respiratory droplets and aerosols, reducing emission at the source and inhalation at the receiving end is physically plausible.

**Observational evidence.** Many observational studies, including analyses of mask requirements in schools, workplaces, and health care settings, found associations between mask use and lower infection rates. Such studies are vulnerable to confounding, however, since mask use often coincided with other precautions.

**Randomized trials.** The evidence here is more mixed. A large cluster-randomized trial in Bangladesh found that promoting surgical masks modestly reduced symptomatic infection, with larger effects among older adults, while the effect of cloth masks was smaller and statistically uncertain. A Danish trial found no statistically significant protection for the wearer alone. A 2023 Cochrane review concluded that community mask wearing probably made little or no difference to respiratory illness, but its authors cautioned that the included trials suffered from low adherence and heterogeneous designs, so the findings should not be read as proof that masks do not work.

**Conclusion.** Masks were most effective when high-quality respirators or surgical masks were worn consistently and correctly, particularly in crowded, enclosed, or high-risk settings, and as one layer of protection alongside vaccination, ventilation, and testing. Their effect at the population level was real but moderate, and it was smaller where compliance was low or masks were poorly fitted.
