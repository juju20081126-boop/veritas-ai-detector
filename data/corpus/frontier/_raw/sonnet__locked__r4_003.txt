You're describing the Monty Hall problem, which is usually told with a car and two goats rather than just goats. Here's the setup and why switching wins more often.

**The setup**

There are three closed doors. Behind one is a car, and behind the other two are goats. You choose a door, say Door 1. The host, who knows what is behind every door, then opens one of the other two doors to reveal a goat. He always opens a door that hides a goat and that you did not pick. He then offers you a choice: stay with your door or switch to the remaining closed one.

Most people think the odds are now 50/50, since two doors remain. That feels natural, but it isn't correct. Switching wins two-thirds of the time, and staying wins only one-third.

**The key idea**

When you first pick, you have a 1 in 3 chance of having the car. That means there's a 2 in 3 chance that the car is behind one of the other two doors. Nothing the host does changes the odds on your original door, because he was always going to open a goat door no matter what you picked. His action doesn't tell you anything about your own door.

But the full 2/3 probability for "the car is somewhere else" doesn't disappear. It gets concentrated onto the one other door that remains closed. So that door now carries a 2/3 chance.

**Walking through the cases**

Suppose you always pick Door 1, and consider the three equally likely situations:

1. **The car is behind Door 1.** The host opens either Door 2 or Door 3. If you switch, you lose.
2. **The car is behind Door 2.** The host must open Door 3. If you switch to Door 2, you win.
3. **The car is behind Door 3.** The host must open Door 2. If you switch to Door 3, you win.

Switching wins in two of the three cases. Staying wins in only one.

**A way to make it intuitive**

Imagine 100 doors. You pick one, and the host opens 98 doors, all of them goats, leaving your door and one other. Would you still think it was 50/50? Your door had a 1 in 100 chance at the start, and the host has essentially pointed to the one door out of the remaining 99 that is most likely to hold the car. Switching clearly makes sense there, and the three-door version works the same way, just with less dramatic numbers.

**The important condition**

The result depends on the host's behavior. He must know where the car is, and he must always open a goat door and offer the switch. If he opened a door at random and it just happened to be a goat, the odds would indeed be 50/50. The solution relies on the host's knowledge, which is what turns his action into useful information.

The puzzle became famous in 1990 when Marilyn vos Savant published the correct answer in a magazine column, and she received thousands of letters, many from people with advanced degrees, insisting that she was wrong. If you're still not convinced, try the experiment with three cards and a friend. After thirty or so rounds, the pattern becomes hard to ignore.

Because light only looks like it travels in straight lines when it interacts with large objects and detectors. In quantum terms, light doesn't follow one path. It explores all possible paths, and each path carries a phase. For most situations, paths that stray from the straight line have phases that vary rapidly and cancel each other out. Only paths near the straight line add up constructively, so the beam appears straight. This is the idea behind Feynman's path integral formulation.

With a double slit, the opening is so small, comparable to the wavelength, that the cancellation fails. Many more paths survive, and they add up in a pattern that produces the bright and dark fringes. A wide opening restores straight-line behavior, and the beam spreads far less. Straight-line propagation is simply the large-scale limit of wave behavior.
