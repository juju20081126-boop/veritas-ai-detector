# How to Make a Great Texas-Style Smoked Brisket

Texas brisket is famously simple on paper: beef, salt, pepper, smoke, and time. The difficulty lies in executing each step well over a cook that can last 12 hours or more. Here's how to do it right.

## 1. Choose the Right Brisket

Buy a **whole packer brisket**, which includes both the lean "flat" and the fattier "point." Aim for 12 to 16 pounds. Grade matters: USDA Prime or high Choice has more intramuscular fat, which makes the meat more forgiving. When you pick it up, a good brisket should bend a little in the middle; a stiff one is often tougher.

## 2. Trim Carefully

Trimming shapes how the brisket cooks.

- Chill the brisket first; cold fat is easier to cut.
- Trim the fat cap down to about **¼ inch**. Too much fat won't render and blocks the bark from forming; too little leaves the meat exposed.
- Remove the hard, waxy fat between the point and the flat (the "deckle") where you can reach it.
- Round off thin edges and corners so they don't burn.
- Square up the flat so it's an even thickness from end to end.

Keep the trimmings. You can render them into tallow for later.

## 3. Season Like Central Texas

The classic rub is just **coarse kosher salt and 16-mesh black pepper**, usually in equal parts by volume. Some pitmasters add a little garlic powder, but purists stick to salt and pepper. Apply an even coat on all sides. If you like, use a thin layer of mustard or hot sauce as a binder, though it isn't necessary. Season an hour before cooking or the night before.

## 4. Set Up Your Smoker

An offset smoker is traditional, but pellet grills, kamados, and kettles set up for indirect heat all work.

- **Wood:** Post oak is the Texas standard. Hickory or pecan are good alternatives. Avoid mesquite for long cooks; it can turn bitter.
- **Temperature:** Hold steady at **225–275°F**. Many pitmasters aim for around 250°F.
- **Smoke:** You want thin, blue, barely visible smoke. Thick white smoke means the fire is smoldering and will coat the meat in a sooty, acrid flavor.

Place a water pan in the cooker to help maintain humidity and stabilize temperature.

## 5. The Cook

Put the brisket on the grate **fat side up or down** depending on your smoker. In offsets where heat comes from one side, many cooks place the point toward the firebox, since it can handle more heat.

Leave it alone for the first several hours. Spritz with water, apple cider vinegar, or beef broth every hour or so after the bark has set (around hour three) if the surface looks dry.

## 6. Beat the Stall

Somewhere around **150–170°F internal**, the temperature will stop climbing, sometimes for hours. This is evaporative cooling. You have two choices:

- **Push through** unwrapped for maximum bark.
- **Wrap** in pink butcher paper (the "Texas crutch") once the bark is a deep mahogany color and doesn't rub off when touched. Butcher paper breathes better than foil, so it preserves more bark while speeding up the cook. Some people add a smear of beef tallow before wrapping.

## 7. Cook to Tenderness, Not a Number

Brisket is usually done between **200 and 205°F**, but temperature is only a guide. The real test is feel: a probe or skewer should slide into the thickest part of the flat with almost no resistance, like pushing into soft butter. Check several spots. If any area feels tight, keep cooking.

## 8. Rest It

Resting is non-negotiable. Pull the brisket, leave it wrapped, and let it rest **at least one hour**, ideally two to four. Wrap the whole package in towels and place it in an empty cooler, or hold it in an oven set to around 150–170°F. Resting lets the juices redistribute and the connective tissue finish relaxing.

## 9. Slice It Properly

- Separate the point from the flat along the fat seam.
- Slice the **flat against the grain** into pencil-thick slices, about ¼ inch.
- Turn the point 90 degrees (its grain runs differently) and slice it slightly thicker, or cube it for burnt ends.

A perfectly cooked slice should hold together when lifted but pull apart with a gentle tug.

## 10. Serve Simply

In Texas, brisket comes on butcher paper with white bread, pickles, raw onion, and maybe sauce on the side. If you've done everything right, you won't need the sauce.

## Quick Tips

- Buy a good leave-in thermometer for both the meat and the pit.
- Plan for roughly **1 to 1.5 hours per pound**, then add time for resting. Start earlier than you think.
- Keep notes on each cook. Brisket rewards practice more than any single recipe.

Plants have no nervous system, no brain, and no way to move from place to place, yet they constantly sense their surroundings and adjust their behavior in response. Over the past few decades, researchers have found that plants exchange information both within their own bodies and with other organisms through several channels. The word "communication" is used loosely here: it means sending and receiving signals that change behavior, not conscious intention.

## Communication Within a Single Plant

**Electrical signals.** When a leaf is wounded, electrical changes in membrane potential can travel to distant parts of the plant within seconds to minutes. In a widely cited 2018 study, Toyota and colleagues used a fluorescent calcium sensor in *Arabidopsis* to show waves of calcium moving from a caterpillar-damaged leaf to other leaves, triggering defense responses. The Venus flytrap is the most dramatic example: two touches to its trigger hairs within about 20 seconds generate action potentials that snap the trap shut.

**Hormonal signals.** Plant hormones such as jasmonic acid, salicylic acid, ethylene, and auxin coordinate growth and defense across tissues. Damage in one leaf can lead to jasmonate-driven production of defensive compounds throughout the plant, a phenomenon called systemic acquired resistance (or, for wound responses, systemic induced resistance).

**Hydraulic signals.** Changes in water pressure can also travel quickly through the vascular system and influence processes like stomatal closure.

## Communication Between Plants

**Airborne chemicals (volatile organic compounds).** This is the best-supported form of plant-to-plant signaling.

- In the 1980s, researchers reported that willow and poplar trees near insect-damaged neighbors increased their own chemical defenses. These early studies were criticized for their methods but sparked a field of research.
- Later, stronger work came from sagebrush and wild tobacco. Richard Karban's field experiments showed that clipping sagebrush released volatiles that made nearby plants (including tobacco) suffer less herbivore damage.
- Lima beans exposed to volatiles from damaged neighbors produce more extrafloral nectar, which attracts predatory insects.
- Green leaf volatiles, the "cut grass" smell, are released almost instantly upon damage and can prime defenses in neighboring plants.

**Root exudates.** Plants release chemicals from their roots that affect neighbors. Some suppress competitors (allelopathy, as in black walnut's juglone). Others appear to help plants recognize kin; studies on sea rocket (*Cakile edentula*) found that plants grew fewer competitive roots when sharing soil with siblings than with strangers.

**Mycorrhizal networks.** Most plants partner with fungi that colonize their roots, and fungal threads can connect multiple plants underground. Experiments in the 1990s by Suzanne Simard and colleagues tracked carbon isotopes moving between trees through these networks, and later lab work suggested that defense signals could travel between connected plants. This idea, often called the "wood wide web," is popular but contested. A 2023 review by Karst, Jones, and Hoeksema argued that field evidence for widespread resource sharing and "mother trees" nurturing seedlings is much weaker than public accounts suggest. Fungal networks clearly exist; how much they function as communication systems is still debated.

## Communication With Other Species

Plants also send signals to animals and microbes:

- **Calling bodyguards:** Corn attacked by caterpillars releases volatiles that attract parasitic wasps, which lay eggs inside the caterpillars.
- **Attracting pollinators:** Flower color, scent, ultraviolet patterns, and even electric fields help guide bees. Some research suggests bumblebees can detect floral electric charges.
- **Recruiting microbes:** Under stress, roots can change their exudates to recruit beneficial bacteria.

## Sensing and Responding

Plants also "listen." Studies have shown that *Arabidopsis* exposed to recordings of caterpillar chewing vibrations later produced more defensive chemicals than plants exposed to silence or wind sounds. Roots of pea plants have been reported to grow toward the sound of running water, though such findings need further replication.

## How Strong Is the Evidence?

| Mechanism | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|
| Internal electrical, hormonal, hydraulic signaling | Strong |
| Airborne volatiles between plants | Strong, especially in lab and some field settings |
| Signals to insects and pollinators | Strong |
| Root-based kin recognition | Moderate |
| Mycorrhizal "communication" and resource sharing | Debated; real but probably overstated |
| Plant "hearing" | Early and intriguing |

## Bottom Line

Plants coordinate their behavior through electrical, chemical, and hydraulic signals, and they exchange chemical information with neighbors, insects, and microbes. These processes are well documented and help plants defend themselves, compete, and reproduce. Claims that plants are "intelligent" or that forests act as cooperative superorganisms go beyond the evidence, but the core finding is solid: plants are far more responsive and interconnected than they appear.
