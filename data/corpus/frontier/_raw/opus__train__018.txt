A fractal is a geometric object that displays self-similarity, meaning that its parts resemble the whole at different scales of magnification, and whose complexity is measured by a fractional, or non-integer, dimension. Although fractals began as abstract constructions in pure mathematics, Benoit Mandelbrot argued in *The Fractal Geometry of Nature* (1982) that they describe many natural forms more faithfully than the smooth figures of Euclidean geometry. Clouds, as he observed, are not spheres, and mountains are not cones.

Coastlines offer the classic illustration. Because a coastline is irregular at every scale, its measured length increases as the measuring unit becomes smaller, a result known as the coastline paradox. Fractal dimension provides a more meaningful description than length alone; the west coast of Great Britain, for example, has a dimension of approximately 1.25.

Living systems frequently adopt fractal architectures because such structures maximize surface area or coverage within a limited volume. The branching of the human airways and blood vessels, like that of trees and root systems, enables the efficient exchange and distribution of gases, fluids, and nutrients.

Fractal patterns also emerge from physical processes, as seen in lightning discharges, mineral dendrites, river networks shaped by erosion, turbulent flow, and the strange attractors that characterize chaotic systems such as atmospheric convection.

It should be noted, however, that physical fractals are approximations. Their self-similarity is typically statistical rather than exact and holds only across a limited range of scales. Even so, the concept has considerable practical value in antenna design, materials science, and computer graphics.

A standard chessboard has **64** squares if you count only the individual light and dark squares. If you count every square that can be traced on the board, including larger squares made up of smaller ones, the answer is **204**.

## Counting squares of every size

In addition to the 1 × 1 squares, the 8 × 8 grid contains 2 × 2 squares, 3 × 3 squares, and so on, up to the entire board, which is itself one 8 × 8 square. The key is to count how many positions each size can occupy.

Take a 2 × 2 square. Its top-left corner can sit in any of the first 7 columns and any of the first 7 rows, so there are 7 × 7 = 49 of them. In general, a k × k square fits in (9 − k)² positions:

| Square size | How many |
|---|---|
| 1 × 1 | 8² = 64 |
| 2 × 2 | 7² = 49 |
| 3 × 3 | 6² = 36 |
| 4 × 4 | 5² = 25 |
| 5 × 5 | 4² = 16 |
| 6 × 6 | 3² = 9 |
| 7 × 7 | 2² = 4 |
| 8 × 8 | 1² = 1 |
| **Total** | **204** |

## The general formula

The total is the sum of the first eight perfect squares, and there's a well-known formula for the sum of the first n squares:

1² + 2² + 3² + ... + n² = n(n + 1)(2n + 1) / 6

With n = 8, this gives (8 × 9 × 17) / 6 = 1,224 / 6 = 204, matching the table. The formula works for any square grid. A 3 × 3 tic-tac-toe board, for example, has 9 + 4 + 1 = 14 squares.

## Going further: rectangles

The puzzle is sometimes extended to rectangles. Every rectangle on the board is defined by picking two of the 9 horizontal grid lines and two of the 9 vertical grid lines. There are C(9, 2) = 36 ways to choose each pair, so the board contains 36 × 36 = **1,296** rectangles, 204 of which are squares.

## Summary

- **64** individual squares
- **204** squares of all sizes
- **1,296** rectangles of all sizes, squares included

The question is a classic precisely because most people stop at 64. For a complete answer, say which interpretation you're using and show the sum that leads to 204.
