This is the **Monty Hall problem**, named after the host of the American game show *Let's Make a Deal*. It's famous because the correct answer feels wrong to almost everyone at first.

## The setup

- There are three closed doors. Behind one is a car; behind the other two are goats.
- You pick a door, say Door 1.
- The host, who **knows** where the car is, opens one of the other two doors and always reveals a goat. Say he opens Door 3.
- He then offers you a choice: stick with Door 1 or switch to Door 2.

Most people reason that two doors remain, so it's 50/50 and switching doesn't matter. In fact, switching wins two times out of three.

## Why switching is better

When you first choose, you have a 1/3 chance of picking the car and a 2/3 chance of picking a goat. Nothing the host does afterward changes what's behind your original door, so your door still has a 1/3 chance.

Look at each possible starting point:

1. **You originally picked the car (probability 1/3).** The host opens either goat door. If you switch, you get a goat. You lose.
2. **You originally picked goat A (probability 1/3).** The host must open the door with goat B, because he can't reveal the car. The remaining door has the car. If you switch, you win.
3. **You originally picked goat B (probability 1/3).** The mirror image: the host reveals goat A, and switching takes you to the car.

Switching wins in two of the three equally likely cases; staying wins in only one. So your chance of winning is 2/3 if you switch and 1/3 if you stay.

## Why the host's knowledge matters

The host's action isn't random. He is forced to avoid the car. Whenever your first pick was a goat, which happens most of the time, the host effectively points you to the car by eliminating the only other goat. That information gets concentrated on the single remaining door.

If the host opened a door at random and just happened to reveal a goat, the odds really would be 50/50. It's the guaranteed, informed reveal that creates the advantage.

## An intuition pump: 100 doors

Imagine 100 doors with one car. You pick Door 1. The host, who knows where the car is, opens 98 other doors, all goats, leaving only Door 1 and, say, Door 57.

Would you really believe your random first pick has the same chance as the one door the host carefully avoided opening? Your door had a 1/100 chance from the start. The other door effectively stands in for all 99 doors you didn't pick, so it carries a 99/100 chance.

The three-door version works the same way, just less dramatically: your door keeps its 1/3, and the other unopened door inherits the remaining 2/3.

## Try it yourself

If you're still skeptical, you're in good company. When Marilyn vos Savant published the correct answer in 1990, she received thousands of letters, many from people with PhDs, insisting she was wrong. The easiest way to convince yourself is to play it with three playing cards and a friend acting as host. After 30 or so rounds, the pattern becomes hard to ignore.

The intuition is that gears are rigid, so turning one end should instantly turn the other. But no material is perfectly rigid. When you rotate the first gear, its atoms push on neighboring atoms through electromagnetic forces, those push on the next atoms, and so on. That push travels through the metal as a compression or shear wave, which moves at the speed of sound in that material.

In steel, sound travels at roughly 5 to 6 kilometers per second. That's fast by everyday standards, but light covers about 300,000 kilometers per second, around 50,000 times faster. Electrical signals in a copper wire propagate as electromagnetic waves guided by the conductor, typically at 50 to 99 percent of light speed.

Gears add further delays. Each meshing pair has a little slack, called backlash, that must be taken up before the next gear moves, and every tooth and shaft twists slightly under load. Over a long chain, these small lags accumulate.

So for a gear chain spanning 1,000 kilometers, the far end would start moving more than three minutes late, assuming the gears didn't strip or the shafts didn't snap first.
