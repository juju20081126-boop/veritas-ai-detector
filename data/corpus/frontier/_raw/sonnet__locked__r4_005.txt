# The Tuner of Halvern Street

Odile had been a piano tuner for thirty-one years before she realized that she had never once tuned a person.

It happened on a Tuesday, in the kitchen of a widower named Mr. Abasi, who had called about his late wife's upright. She struck middle C and frowned. The piano was fine. The man standing behind her, holding a teapot with both hands, was the thing out of true.

She heard it the way she heard everything: as a slight beating between two frequencies that should have agreed. His voice said he was well. His shoulders said something a half-step lower.

"Would you play something?" she asked.

"I don't play."

"Neither do I, really. I just listen."

He set down the teapot. Odile sat on the bench and pressed a single low note, letting it ring until the room hummed with it. Mr. Abasi's breath caught. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed, and the laugh had a crack running through it like a hairline in a soundboard, and then the crack opened and he was crying into a dish towel.

She didn't hurry him. You never hurry a string. Turn the pin too fast and it snaps.

Over the following months Odile noticed it everywhere. Her neighbor Pell was a cello, all long patient silences broken by a sudden, rich vibration when someone said something kind. The bus driver was a brass section, loud and a little sharp but warm underneath. Her niece, who was nine, was a xylophone struck by a dozen mallets at once, delightfully out of tune with herself and not at all troubled by it.

And Odile herself? She had never thought to ask. She tuned the pianos of others and left her own life with a sticky pedal and a stuck key in the upper register.

It was Mr. Abasi who answered, six months later, when he turned up at her workshop with a loaf of bread and a pair of tickets.

"You're a tuning fork," he said, as though it were obvious. "You don't make the music. You just hold one true note so everyone else can find their way back to it."

Odile turned the tickets over in her hand. It was a concert, a small one, in a church hall across town. Nothing grand.

"I can't promise I'll enjoy it," she said. "I'll be too busy hearing everything wrong."

"Then come and hear everything right, for once. Sit next to someone who's only a little out of tune."

She went. In the third row of a drafty hall, beside a man who smelled of woodsmoke and bread, she listened to a clumsy amateur quartet saw its way through Schubert. Every note was slightly off, and she did not mind at all. When the cellist reached the long, aching phrase in the second movement, Mr. Abasi's hand found hers on the armrest, and for the first time in thirty-one years, something in Odile rang out clean and clear.
