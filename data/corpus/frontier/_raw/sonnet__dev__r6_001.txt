## Why Kuzushi Comes First

Judo was founded by Jigoro Kano in 1882 on the principle of "maximum efficiency, minimum effort." Kano observed that a person who is off balance can be thrown with very little force, while a person who is firmly balanced can resist even a much stronger attacker. Kuzushi is the application of that idea. Before you attempt any throw, you pull, push, or move your opponent so that their weight is no longer over their feet.

Kano described this in terms of three stages: kuzushi (breaking balance), tsukuri (fitting in, or positioning your body for the technique), and kake (the execution of the throw). Many beginners focus on kake, because that is the dramatic part. Experienced players spend most of their effort on the first two stages. A throw that is started without kuzushi usually fails, or it succeeds only through brute strength, which defeats the point of judo.

Kuzushi also applies beyond throwing. In newaza, or groundwork, you disrupt your opponent's posture and base before applying a pin or submission. In grip fighting, the whole contest is about who can control the other's balance first. So it is a skill that runs through the entire sport rather than belonging to one technique.

## Strong Alternatives

**Grip fighting (kumi kata).** At high levels of competition, many matches are decided by grips. A fighter who gets the dominant grip can attack freely, while one who cannot is forced into defence. Some coaches say that whoever wins the grip fight wins the match, and the rules changes of recent years have made this even more important.

**Seoi nage.** This shoulder throw is probably the most widely used technique in competitive judo. It is versatile, works for both tall and short athletes, and can be adapted into many variations. If you ask which single throw to learn first, many instructors will say this one.

**Uchi mata.** The inner thigh throw is a favourite of elite players, because it is powerful and can be linked into other attacks. It requires good timing, flexibility, and excellent balance of your own.

**Ukemi (breakfalls).** It may seem strange to count falling as a skill, but ukemi is arguably the foundation of the whole art. A judoka who can fall safely can train hard, take risks, and avoid injury. Without it, nobody could practice throws at full speed, so every other skill depends on it.

**Newaza.** Ground fighting is where many matches end, and a strong groundwork specialist can beat a better thrower by getting the fight to the mat and holding or submitting the opponent.

## How to Decide

Which skill is "best" depends on what you want from judo. For competition, grip fighting and a strong signature throw matter a great deal. For self-defence, breakfalls and balance-breaking are probably the most valuable. For personal development, the principle of using your opponent's energy rather than fighting against it is the lesson that stays with people long after they have stopped competing.

## Conclusion

I would choose kuzushi as the best skill because it is the foundation of every throw, pin, and transition, and because it reflects the core philosophy of judo. Without it, technique becomes a contest of strength. With it, a smaller player can handle a bigger one. Ukemi and grip fighting are close behind, and a well-rounded judoka needs all of them. - **Soccer (football):** the world's most popular sport, played with a spherical ball that is moved mainly with the feet.
- **Basketball:** players shoot the ball through a hoop and dribble with their hands.
- **Volleyball:** two teams hit the ball over a net, trying to make it land on the opponent's side.
- **Handball:** players pass and throw the ball and try to score in a goal.
- **Water polo:** played in a pool, with players throwing the ball into a goal while treading water.
- **Netball:** similar to basketball, but players can't dribble or run with the ball.
- **Baseball and softball:** a pitcher throws a ball to a batter, who tries to hit it and run around the bases.
- **Cricket:** a bat-and-ball game with a hard leather ball, popular in countries such as India, England, and Australia.
- **Field hockey and lacrosse** use a ball too, though players hit or carry it with a stick.

**Racquet and individual sports**
- **Tennis:** a felt-covered ball is hit across a net with a racquet.
- **Table tennis:** a small, light ball is played on a table.
- **Badminton** uses a shuttlecock, so it doesn't count, but **squash** and **racquetball** do use balls.
- **Golf:** players hit a small ball into holes using clubs, with the fewest strokes winning.
- **Bowling** and **bocce:** players roll balls toward pins or a target ball.
- **Billiards, pool, and snooker:** cue sports played with balls on a table.

Rugby and American football use oval balls, so they're not on this list.
