**Should Universities Teach Skills or Theory?**

Every year, millions of students walk into university lecture halls with a question in the back of their minds: what will all this lead to? For some, the answer should be a good job. For others, it should be a better way of thinking. The debate over whether universities should teach practical job skills or theoretical knowledge touches on what higher education is really for.

People who favor practical skills have a strong argument. University is expensive, and graduates often leave with significant debt. They want assurance that their degree will lead to employment. Courses that teach coding, accounting, nursing or engineering practice can give students abilities they can use on the first day of work. Internships and project-based classes also help students understand what a profession is actually like before they commit to it. Employers, too, often complain that new graduates arrive knowing plenty of facts but little about how to apply them.

On the other side, supporters of theoretical knowledge argue that specific skills expire quickly. The software a company uses today might be replaced in five years, and a student who learned only that tool could be left behind. Theory, by contrast, teaches principles that last. Studying mathematics, philosophy, history or economics trains students to analyze problems, question assumptions and build arguments. These abilities are useful in nearly every career, including ones that don't exist yet. Universities also have a mission beyond employment: they push human understanding forward, and that requires research grounded in theory.

I believe the best approach combines both, with theory as the base. A student who understands why a method works can adapt when the method changes, and a student who has practiced applying ideas will be more confident when facing real situations. Universities can achieve this by pairing lectures with labs, case studies and work placements. Neither extreme serves students well. A purely practical education produces workers who can follow instructions but not adjust, while a purely theoretical one risks producing graduates who know a great deal but have never used it.

In the end, a university's job is to prepare people for both a career and a life. Teaching students how to think and how to do should not be competing goals. Together, they give graduates the best chance of succeeding in a changing world.

The use of special symbols in pronunciation guides reflects a fundamental limitation of the English writing system. English spelling is not phonetic. A single letter can represent several sounds, as with the "a" in "cat," "father" and "late," and a single sound can be written in multiple ways. The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) was developed to give each distinct sound exactly one symbol, so that pronunciation could be recorded without ambiguity.

Adding those symbols to the English alphabet would not be practical. Many of them, such as the schwa or the symbol for the "th" sound, would require readers and writers to learn new characters, and printing, keyboards and existing literature would need to be adapted. English spelling also preserves historical and etymological information, which makes reform politically and culturally difficult. Moreover, pronunciation varies by region, so no single set of respellings would suit every speaker. For these reasons, the symbols remain a specialized tool for dictionaries and linguists rather than part of everyday writing.
