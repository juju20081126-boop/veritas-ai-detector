**Plausible Deniability**

Not fame. Not fortune. Honesty, delivered at age four, with enormous eyes and a lisp.

My debut was Great-Aunt Marjorie's funeral. Mom had stuffed me into a clip-on tie. I walked up to the open casket, studied the body with great seriousness, and announced to the silent chapel, "She looks way nicer now. Mommy said she was mean when she was awake."

Mom made a noise like a kettle. A cousin began coughing into a hymnal. I held up my juice box and asked, "Is there still going to be cake?"

I was hooked.

Over the next year I refined my craft. The rules were simple. Never be crude, because crude gets you punished. Be curious. Be sweet. Be wrong in precisely the right place.

At the grocery store, I told a man with a very ambitious toupee, "Your hair is trying to run away, but it's stuck." At my Uncle Rob's third wedding, I whispered at full volume, "Do I have to learn this lady's name, or is she leaving too?" My parents sent me to a child therapist with a lovely toy box. I spent the session staring at the pale stripe on her ring finger and asked, with real sympathy, whether it was hard being single again. She ended our appointment early.

Then came kindergarten. Miss Alvarez gathered us on a rainbow carpet for sharing time.

"Teddy," she said gently, "tell us something about yourself."

I stood and folded my hands. "I'm Teddy. Mommy says you only wear that lipstick when Principal Dunn is around, but he's married, so I don't think it's going to work."

Miss Alvarez went the color of a boiled shrimp. A boy in the front row began to cry. It was, I felt, my finest work.

I turned around. A girl in a cardigan and sensible shoes sat cross-legged on the carpet, regarding me the way a chess player regards a rival. She didn't smile. She nodded once, slowly, like someone at a funeral.

At recess she found me by the swings and offered a tiny hand. "Beatrice. Former HR director. Choked on a pretzel at my own retirement party."

"Teddy. Claims adjuster."

Her grip tightened. "Oh, I know," she said sweetly. "You denied my mother's roof claim in 1994."

Across the playground, Miss Alvarez watched us shake hands and sighed to the aide beside her. "Aww. They're already friends."

Beatrice squeezed until something in my hand cracked. It was, without question, the best day of my second life.

A fractal is a mathematical object that exhibits detail at every scale of magnification, usually through self-similarity, and whose fractal dimension need not be an integer. Although ideal fractals such as the Koch curve exist only in theory, many physical systems approximate fractal geometry over a finite range of scales, which makes the concept a valuable descriptive tool.

Natural structures provide the clearest examples. Coastlines, river networks, mountain ranges, and clouds display irregular, repeating patterns whose measured length or area depends on the scale of measurement, a property Benoit Mandelbrot famously analyzed. Biological systems show similar organization: the branching of bronchial passages and blood vessels packs enormous surface area into limited volume, supporting efficient exchange and transport.

Fractal geometry also emerges from physical processes. Diffusion-limited aggregation produces branching clusters resembling lightning and electrodeposition patterns, while chaotic dynamical systems often evolve on strange attractors with fractal structure. Scale-invariant behavior near critical points in phase transitions is likewise closely related to fractal scaling.

These ideas have practical value. Fractal analysis informs antenna design, image compression, medical diagnostics, and the modeling of porous materials and terrain.

The analogy has limits, however. Physical objects are fractal only across a bounded range of scales, constrained by atomic dimensions at one extreme and by the size of the system at the other. Fractal models should therefore be regarded as effective approximations rather than literal descriptions. Their ability to quantify irregularity nonetheless explains their enduring relevance across the physical sciences.

The woman in the mirror had my hair, my chipped tooth, my scar through the left eyebrow. But one of her eyes, brown all my life, had turned gray. Tomas's gray.

I stepped closer, and the rest surfaced like watermarks. My mother's stubborn jaw. My brother's freckles. The crooked half smile Tomas wore whenever he was about to apologize. Everyone I had lost was in my face, layered there, faint and patient, looking back.

That was when I understood. The vaccine hadn't poisoned anyone. It had made room for everyone we'd ever lost, and the ones who died and the ones who screamed had simply been unable to look.

I looked until I could bear it. Then I looked a little longer.

"Hello," I whispered.

Every one of them smiled back.

A standard 8×8 chessboard has 64 individual squares, but if you count squares of every size, including the larger ones formed by groups of cells, the total is **204**. This is a classic counting puzzle, and the key is to count systematically by size.

## Counting by size

A k×k square can sit in (9 - k) × (9 - k) different positions on the board, so you can count each size separately:

| Square size | Positions | Number of squares |
|---|---|---|
| 1×1 | 8 × 8 | 64 |
| 2×2 | 7 × 7 | 49 |
| 3×3 | 6 × 6 | 36 |
| 4×4 | 5 × 5 | 25 |
| 5×5 | 4 × 4 | 16 |
| 6×6 | 3 × 3 | 9 |
| 7×7 | 2 × 2 | 4 |
| 8×8 | 1 × 1 | 1 |

Adding these up: 64 + 49 + 36 + 25 + 16 + 9 + 4 + 1 = **204**.

## A general formula

The totals form a sum of consecutive perfect squares. For an n×n board, the number of squares is:

1² + 2² + 3² + … + n² = n(n + 1)(2n + 1) / 6

For n = 8, this gives (8 × 9 × 17) / 6 = 1224 / 6 = 204, which matches the table. The same formula gives 5 squares on a 2×2 grid (four small squares and one large one), 14 on a 3×3 grid, 30 on a 4×4 grid, and 55 on a 5×5 grid.

## Why the formula works

One way to see it is to count squares by their bottom-right corner. A corner in row i and column j can anchor min(i, j) different squares, since the square cannot extend past the edge of the board. Summing min(i, j) over every cell of an n×n board produces the same total as the formula above.

## A related question

If the question were about rectangles, the answer would be different. Choosing 2 of the 9 horizontal lines and 2 of the 9 vertical lines gives 36 × 36 = 1,296 rectangles, and 204 of those are squares.

So the answer depends on what you count: 64 individual cells, or 204 squares of all sizes.
