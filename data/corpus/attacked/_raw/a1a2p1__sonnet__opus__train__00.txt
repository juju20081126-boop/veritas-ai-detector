Plants lack a nervous system, a brain, and any means of moving from place to place, but they continually sense what is around them and change their behavior accordingly. Over the last few decades, researchers have discovered that plants pass information both within their own bodies and to other organisms through several channels. The word "communication" is used loosely here: it refers to sending and receiving signals that alter behavior, not to conscious intent.

## Communication Within a Single Plant

**Electrical signals.** When a leaf is injured, changes in membrane potential can spread to distant parts of the plant within seconds to minutes. In a widely cited 2018 study, Toyota and colleagues used a fluorescent calcium sensor in *Arabidopsis* to reveal waves of calcium traveling from a caterpillar-damaged leaf to other leaves and setting off defense responses. The Venus flytrap is the most dramatic case: two touches to its trigger hairs within roughly 20 seconds produce action potentials that make the trap snap shut.

**Hormonal signals.** Plant hormones including jasmonic acid, salicylic acid, ethylene, and auxin coordinate growth and defense between tissues. Damage to one leaf can cause jasmonate-driven production of defensive compounds across the whole plant, a phenomenon called systemic acquired resistance (or, for wound responses, systemic induced resistance).

**Hydraulic signals.** Shifts in water pressure can also move rapidly through the vascular system and affect processes such as stomatal closure.

## Communication Between Plants

**Airborne chemicals (volatile organic compounds).** This is the best-supported kind of plant-to-plant signaling.

- In the 1980s, researchers reported that willow and poplar trees growing near insect-damaged neighbors raised their own chemical defenses. These early studies drew criticism over their methods but started a new field of research.
- Stronger evidence arrived later from sagebrush and wild tobacco. Richard Karban's field experiments showed that clipping sagebrush released volatiles that left nearby plants (tobacco included) with less herbivore damage.
- Lima beans exposed to volatiles from damaged neighbors make more extrafloral nectar, which draws predatory insects.
- Green leaf volatiles, the "cut grass" smell, are released almost immediately after damage and can prime the defenses of neighboring plants.

**Root exudates.** Plants release chemicals from their roots that affect nearby plants. Some hold back competitors (allelopathy, as with juglone from black walnut). Others seem to help plants recognize relatives; studies of sea rocket (*Cakile edentula*) found that plants grew fewer competitive roots when they shared soil with siblings than when they shared it with strangers.

**Mycorrhizal networks.** Most plants form partnerships with fungi that colonize their roots, and fungal threads can link several plants underground. In the 1990s, Suzanne Simard and colleagues ran experiments that followed carbon isotopes moving between trees through these networks, and later laboratory work suggested that defense signals might pass between connected plants. This idea, often called the "wood wide web," is popular but disputed. A 2023 review by Karst, Jones, and Hoeksema argued that field evidence for widespread resource sharing and for "mother trees" nurturing seedlings is much weaker than popular accounts suggest. Fungal networks plainly exist; how far they work as communication systems is still debated.

## Communication With Other Species

Plants also send signals to animals and microbes:

- **Calling bodyguards:** Corn attacked by caterpillars releases volatiles that draw parasitic wasps, which lay their eggs inside the caterpillars.
- **Attracting pollinators:** Flower color, scent, ultraviolet patterns, and even electric fields help steer bees. Some research suggests that bumblebees can sense floral electric charges.
- **Recruiting microbes:** When stressed, roots can alter their exudates to recruit helpful bacteria.

## Sensing and Responding

Plants also "listen." Studies have found that *Arabidopsis* exposed to recordings of caterpillar chewing vibrations later made more defensive chemicals than plants exposed to silence or wind sounds. Pea roots have reportedly grown toward the sound of running water, although such findings need further replication.

## How Strong Is the Evidence?

| Mechanism | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|
| Internal electrical, hormonal, hydraulic signaling | Strong |
| Airborne volatiles between plants | Strong, especially in lab and some field settings |
| Signals to insects and pollinators | Strong |
| Root-based kin recognition | Moderate |
| Mycorrhizal "communication" and resource sharing | Debated; real but probably overstated |
| Plant "hearing" | Early and intriguing |

## Bottom Line

Plants coordinate their behavior with electrical, chemical, and hydraulic signals, and they trade chemical information with neighbors, insects, and microbes. These processes are well documented and help plants defend themselves, compete, and reproduce. Claims that plants are "intelligent" or that forests behave as cooperative superorganisms go further than the evidence allows, but the central finding is solid: plants are much more responsive and interconnected than they look.

# How to Cook an Excellent Texas-Style Smoked Brisket

On paper, Texas brisket is famously simple: beef, salt, pepper, smoke, and time. The hard part is doing every step well across a cook that may run 12 hours or longer. Here is how to get it right.

## 1. Pick the Right Brisket

Get a **whole packer brisket**, which contains both the lean "flat" and the fattier "point," and look for one between 12 and 16 pounds. The grade makes a difference: USDA Prime or top Choice carries more intramuscular fat, so the meat forgives mistakes more easily. A good brisket should sag a little in the middle when you lift it, while a rigid one is often tougher.

## 2. Trim With Care

How you trim determines how the brisket cooks.

- Chill the brisket beforehand, because cold fat cuts more easily.
- Cut the fat cap down to roughly **¼ inch**. If it is too thick, the fat will not render and will keep the bark from forming; if it is too thin, the meat is left exposed.
- Take out the hard, waxy fat between the point and the flat (the "deckle") wherever you can get at it.
- Round off thin edges and corners so they won't burn.
- Square off the flat so its thickness is uniform from end to end.

Hold on to the trimmings, since you can render them into tallow later.

## 3. Season the Central Texas Way

The traditional rub is nothing more than **coarse kosher salt and 16-mesh black pepper**, normally in equal volumes. Some pitmasters put in a little garlic powder, but purists keep to salt and pepper. Coat every side evenly. A thin film of mustard or hot sauce can act as a binder if you like, though you don't need it. You can season an hour before cooking or the night before.

## 4. Prepare Your Smoker

An offset smoker is the traditional choice, but pellet grills, kamados, and kettle grills arranged for indirect heat all do the job.

- **Wood:** Post oak is the Texas standard, with hickory or pecan as good substitutes. Skip mesquite for long cooks, as it can go bitter.
- **Temperature:** Keep it steady between **225 and 275°F**. Many pitmasters target about 250°F.
- **Smoke:** Look for thin, blue smoke that you can barely see. Thick white smoke signals a smoldering fire and will leave a sooty, bitter taste on the meat.

Set a water pan inside the cooker to keep the humidity up and the temperature steady.

## 5. The Cook

Lay the brisket on the grate **fat side up or down**, depending on your smoker. In offsets, where the heat enters from one side, many cooks aim the point toward the firebox because it tolerates more heat.

Don't disturb it for the first several hours. Once the bark has set (around hour three), spritz with water, apple cider vinegar, or beef broth about once an hour whenever the surface looks dry.

## 6. Get Past the Stall

At around **150 to 170°F internal**, the temperature will stop rising, at times for hours. That is evaporative cooling at work. You have two options:

- **Push through** without wrapping to get the most bark.
- **Wrap** in pink butcher paper (the "Texas crutch") once the bark has turned a deep mahogany and no longer rubs off when you touch it. Butcher paper breathes better than foil, so it keeps more of the bark while still shortening the cook. Some cooks spread a little beef tallow on before wrapping.

## 7. Go by Tenderness, Not a Number

Brisket generally finishes between **200 and 205°F**, but the temperature is just a guideline. The real test is feel: a probe or skewer should go into the thickest part of the flat with almost no resistance, as if you were pushing it into soft butter. Test several places, and if any spot feels tight, keep cooking.

## 8. Let It Rest

You can't skip the rest. Take the brisket off, keep it wrapped, and let it sit **for at least an hour**, ideally two to four. Wrap the whole bundle in towels and set it in an empty cooler, or keep it in an oven at about 150 to 170°F. Resting lets the juices spread back through the meat and gives the connective tissue time to finish relaxing.

## 9. Slice It the Right Way

- Split the point from the flat along the fat seam.
- Cut the **flat against the grain** into slices as thick as a pencil, about ¼ inch.
- Rotate the point 90 degrees, since its grain runs in a different direction, and cut it a little thicker, or cube it for burnt ends.

A perfectly cooked slice should stay together when you lift it but come apart with a light tug.

## 10. Keep the Serving Simple

In Texas, brisket arrives on butcher paper with white bread, pickles, raw onion, and perhaps some sauce on the side. If you did everything properly, you won't need the sauce.

## Quick Tips

- Invest in a good leave-in thermometer for both the meat and the pit.
- Allow about **1 to 1.5 hours per pound**, then add resting time. Begin earlier than you think you need to.
- Write down notes on every cook. Brisket rewards practice more than it rewards any single recipe.

Then I shut my eyes over a pile of tax returns and opened them in a hospital nursery, red-faced, furious, and named Tyler, with all my memories still in place.

It took some time before I realized what I had been handed. It wasn't the knowledge; it was the immunity. Nobody blames a small child for anything. Getting my mouth to work properly took five painful years, and I've been making up for the delay ever since.

At Aunt Denise's wedding, in the sacred quiet just before the vows, I pulled on my mother's sleeve and asked, loudly enough to reach the back pew, "Mommy, when they get divorced, who gets the boat?"

At Grandpa Lou's funeral, the pastor asked anyone with a memory to step up. I put my hand in the air. "Grandpa always said he didn't trust banks," I told the mourners, "so is the money still in the freezer, or has somebody already looked?" Three of my uncles left before the last hymn. Only two returned.

When Dad invited his regional manager over for pot roast, I held off until the man's mouth was full, and then I asked if golden parachutes were just for the people who do the firing, and whether Daddy would be getting a parachute or only a shove.

At the mall, I climbed into Santa's lap and requested a Roth IRA. "Mommy says it's too late for her," I explained, "but I still have time to let compound interest do its job." Santa, who I'd guess was sixty-one and just renting the suit, needed a moment.

In Sunday school, Mrs. Abernathy asked what God expects from us. "Ten percent," I answered. "But is that before or after taxes?"

My parents have had me evaluated twice. The first psychologist called me gifted. The second asked, very carefully, whether anyone in our house worked in accounting.

I know what you're thinking. A CPA's mind inside a six-year-old's body ought to make me a prodigy, with morning television appearances and college at eleven. Instead I'm the kid who asked a pregnant stranger in Target whether she planned to claim the baby as a dependent this year.

But I have already lived the sensible life. It earned me a corner office, a retirement party I didn't survive to attend, and a heart attack in the middle of tax season.

Tonight Mom tucked me in and asked, in the voice of a woman at the very end of her rope, where I pick up these things. I gave her my roundest, most innocent eyes.

"Mommy," I whispered, "what's a midlife crisis, and why does Daddy's new car have one?"

A fractal is a geometric object that shows self-similarity, which means its parts look like the whole at different levels of magnification, and its complexity is captured by a fractional, or non-integer, dimension. Fractals started out as abstract constructions in pure mathematics, but Benoit Mandelbrot argued in *The Fractal Geometry of Nature* (1982) that they describe many natural forms more accurately than the smooth shapes of Euclidean geometry. As he put it, clouds are not spheres and mountains are not cones.

Coastlines are the classic example. A coastline is irregular at every scale, so its measured length grows as the unit of measurement shrinks, a result called the coastline paradox. Fractal dimension gives a more meaningful description than length alone; the west coast of Great Britain, for instance, has a dimension of about 1.25.

Living systems often take on fractal designs because such structures maximize surface area or coverage within a limited volume. The branching of human airways and blood vessels, like that of trees and root systems, allows gases, fluids, and nutrients to be exchanged and distributed efficiently.

Fractal patterns also arise from physical processes, as in lightning discharges, mineral dendrites, river networks carved by erosion, turbulent flow, and the strange attractors that mark chaotic systems such as atmospheric convection.

Note, though, that physical fractals are only approximations. Their self-similarity is usually statistical rather than exact, and it holds only across a limited range of scales. Even so, the concept is of real practical value in antenna design, materials science, and computer graphics.

Population atlases of the brain and spinal cord are vital for spatial normalization, morphometric analysis, and research on neurological disease. However, most current atlases handle the brain and spinal cord separately and rely on deterministic registration, which reveals little about anatomical variability. We introduce a generative framework for building diffeomorphic atlases that models the brain and cervical spinal cord together from MRI data. Each subject is expressed as a smooth, invertible deformation of a common template, and both the template and a probabilistic model of the deformations are learned through variational inference, implemented with a convolutional neural network. This formulation makes it possible to register new subjects efficiently, draw samples of plausible anatomical variation, and measure the uncertainty in the estimated deformations. We test the approach on multi-subject T1- and T2-weighted MRI datasets and show better alignment of anatomical structures, preserved topology, and much shorter computation time than conventional iterative atlas-building methods.
