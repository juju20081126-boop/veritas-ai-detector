**What the Mirror Kept**

It was meant to be the end of it. A single dose, they promised, and we would never again spend a winter tallying the dead.

Six weeks later, the world was empty. The fortunate went peacefully, in their sleep. Everyone else lost their minds, murmuring to people no one else could see, until their bodies quit. Tomas, my husband, passed his final four days in the unlit bathroom, saying sorry to the mirror.

By rights I should have gone after him. Same dose, same clinic, same Tuesday. Yet on the fortieth day I woke up lucid, in an apartment that still carried his smell.

Mirrors I stayed away from. I draped a bedsheet over the one in the bathroom and left every other room in darkness. In the empty houses I picked through for supplies, I had come across the mad curled before mirrors, hands pressed flat to the glass, as though they meant to crawl inside.

After another week, I had no excuses left. I tugged the sheet down and looked.

The woman looking back had my hair, my chipped tooth, the scar that cut through my left eyebrow. But one of her eyes, which had been brown my whole life, was now gray. The gray of Tomas's eyes.

When I leaned in, the rest rose to the surface like watermarks. The stubborn set of my mother's jaw. My brother's freckles. That lopsided half smile Tomas always had just before he said sorry. Every person I had lost was there in my face, layered over it, faint and patient, gazing out at me.

Then it made sense. Nobody had been poisoned by the vaccine. It had opened a space for everyone we had ever lost, and those who died and those who screamed had just been unable to face it.

I kept looking until I could stand it. And then I kept looking a bit more.

"Hello," I said softly.

All of them smiled back at me.

Surface-embedded networks appear in many settings, from the design of transport and communication systems to terrain analysis and geometric modeling. Finding optimal or canonical networks on a surface is hard, since geometric constraints interact with topological ones such as genus, boundary components and homotopy classes. We present a framework that deals with these constraints by moving to general covering spaces of the surface, where the relevant structure becomes easier to represent and work with. A network on the base surface is lifted into the cover, optimized there subject to a symmetry condition, and then projected back down, turning questions about the surface into questions about a space that is easier to handle. We prove that this correspondence keeps both length and connectivity intact, give a characterization of which covers suffice for a particular class of networks, and establish bounds on how large the cover must be. In terms of applications, we derive algorithms that compute shortest networks and Steiner-type networks inside prescribed homotopy classes, and we explain how the same tools carry over to networks that have branching and boundary conditions. In this way, several constructions that were previously treated separately emerge as special cases of a single covering-space principle.

**How a Teenager's Curiosity Brought the FBI to His Door**

As a teenager, Timothy Winslow devoted nearly all his spare hours to a single pursuit: poking around inside computer systems. Sports and the typical teenage hangouts held no appeal for him. He was hooked instead on figuring out how networked machines operated, and he chased that puzzle all over the country, logging into systems belonging to companies, universities and institutions he had never set foot in.

In his own mind, it was simply curiosity. He insists he never regarded it as a crime. He was working out how the systems connected, following links from one computer to the next and testing how far one login could get him. "It was like a giant map that nobody had finished drawing," he remembered.

And then FBI agents showed up.

Their visit transformed what he considered a pastime into a grave legal problem. Under federal law, gaining unauthorized access to computer systems is a crime no matter what the person intended, and investigators could not tell whether an inquisitive teenager might also pose a danger. For a young man who had spent months absorbed in terminals and passwords, it was an abrupt lesson that the outside world has rules his curiosity had overlooked.

Winslow's situation is far from rare. As computers grew essential to commerce and government, a whole generation of young hobbyists learned on their own how to navigate networks, frequently without grasping what could follow. Some ended up prosecuted. Others got off with warnings. A handful were eventually hired by the very organizations whose systems they had explored.

According to security experts, the story highlights an ongoing tension. The abilities that let someone break into a system are exactly those required to protect it, and plenty of current professionals began just as Winslow did. The difference between a career and a criminal record, they contend, frequently depends on permission, mentoring and timing.

Winslow says he has no regrets about what he learned. Still, he wishes someone had shown him sooner where the boundary lay. "I would have been glad to explore the right way," he said, "if I'd known there was one."

Should University Teach Practical Skills or Theory?

A common question is what universities ought to teach. One group believes students need to learn practical skills for work. Another group believes theory matters more. In my view, each side makes good arguments.

To begin with, practical skills are extremely helpful. After graduation, students have to get a job. Employers usually prefer people who can start working right away. A computer science student, for instance, should be able to write actual programs instead of just reading about them. In addition, practical courses such as internships give students real experience. They get to know people at the company and see how an office runs. Because of this, getting a first job becomes easier.

On the other hand, theory also matters. It shows students how to think. If you know why something works, you can figure out new problems on your own. Technology moves very quickly, and a skill that everyone wants now may be outdated in five years. Yet a student who understands the basic principles can pick up new tools fast. Someone who knows economic theory, for example, can understand many kinds of business jobs instead of just one.

Also, university is not just about finding work. It is somewhere to mature, to think about big questions and to discover the world. If universities taught nothing but job skills, students might miss this opportunity.

I believe the best universities should combine the two. In the early years, students need theory to give them a solid foundation. Later, they should take part in projects, practice and internships to apply what they have learned. This way, they will be prepared for work and also able to continue learning later on.

In conclusion, practical skills help students find jobs, while theory helps them build a good long-term career. For me, balancing the two is the best solution.

# Boko Haram Said to Swear Loyalty to ISIS

According to reports, the Nigerian militant group Boko Haram has sworn allegiance to ISIS, a step that may tie together two of the most feared extremist groups in the world, although experts believe the immediate impact on the ground could be small.

If confirmed, the pledge would be an important development for Boko Haram, which for years has conducted bombings, abductions and mass killings across northeastern Nigeria and nearby countries. Internationally, the group is most notorious for kidnapping hundreds of schoolgirls from the town of Chibok. In lining up behind ISIS, which holds territory in Syria and Iraq, Boko Haram seems to be looking for a higher profile within the worldwide jihadist movement.

Analysts point out that declarations like this are often as much about symbolism as about operations. Peter Pham, who specializes in African security issues, said he does not expect the merger to change the military picture in the near term.

"In the short term, the merger won't have a big impact on the battlefield," Pham said, indicating that the difficulty of coordinating over thousands of miles will restrict any immediate consequences. The two groups function in very different settings, and it is not thought that either can quickly send the other large numbers of fighters or significant resources.

Nonetheless, observers believe the pledge may matter more over time. Being linked with ISIS could boost Boko Haram's recruitment and propaganda, and it might eventually lead to greater exchange of tactics, training and money. It could also prompt governments in the region and their international partners to view the danger as one front in the wider struggle against ISIS.

Nigeria and neighboring states have been trying to drive Boko Haram back, yet the group keeps launching deadly attacks. Authorities have so far not commented in detail on the reported pledge, and how ISIS will react to the declaration remains unclear.

"Four out of five dentists recommend" is a marketing claim, and what it actually means hinges on how the survey behind it was carried out. Companies do poll dentists at times, but these samples tend to be small and the questions are often worded vaguely. Dentists might simply be asked if they would recommend a product, or they might be permitted to list more than one brand, so numerous brands can each say they are endorsed by most dentists. As a result, the number does not necessarily mean that 80 percent of dentists favor that particular toothpaste over every other. Shoppers should check the sample size, the precise wording of the question, and whether there is independent endorsement, such as a seal from a recognized dental association.

This work examines kinetic theory models of wealth distribution in a market economy, focusing especially on how power-law tails arise. In a Boltzmann-type setting, agents trade wealth in binary exchanges that mix a conservative part with a stochastic part standing for risk and speculation. From the master equation that follows, we derive approximations of Fokker-Planck type in the regime of small exchanges and find the stationary distributions explicitly. We prove that if the agents are divided into groups that differ in their saving propensity or their level of risk, every group converges to its own exponential or gamma-type equilibrium with a distinct characteristic scale. Wealth across the entire population is therefore a superposition of these exponentials. We show analytically that this overlap, when weighted by an appropriate distribution of scales, produces a Pareto power-law tail whose exponent is set by the statistics of the parameters. Numerical simulations of the corresponding particle system agree with the analytical predictions and display convergence to the stationary profiles. These findings provide a clear mechanism through which heterogeneity among economic agents gives rise to the heavy-tailed wealth distributions seen in empirical data, and they make explicit when the bulk and the tail of the distribution can coexist.

The lights never went out. Teodora kept trying to make the panicking people understand this during that first hour. It wasn't darkness. It was nothing, the loss of something you had never known could be lost.

She had lost her sight at six. Now forty-one, she knew her daily routine by heart, the way others know the words to songs: eleven steps between the bed and the door, the creak of the third stair, the corner bus shelter that smelled of diesel and rain. When everyone else went blind at 8:14 one Tuesday morning, she was already sitting in that shelter, and she heard the screams start.

First came a car horn that kept blaring. Then breaking glass. Then, on the other side of the street, a woman saying "I can't see, I can't see" over and over, like a prayer whose ending she had lost.

Teodora got to her feet. She felt no fear, and she was surprised by how little thought it took.

"This way," she called out in her clear teacher's voice. "Follow my voice. Keep a hand on the wall."

Inside an hour, forty people were crowded into the shelter and the pharmacy next door. By nightfall there were two hundred. They came to her because she was among the few in the city who knew how to get around.

After a week, no one questioned whether she should be in charge.

The sighted had lost everything in a single moment. They walked into doorframes and burned themselves on stoves. They had no idea that an echo tells you how big a room is, or that a cane moved in a slow arc finds the curb before the curb finds you. Teodora and the others, from the old Braille club, from the school on Alder Street, from years of patient, quiet practice, had to become teachers. They showed people how to count steps, how to mark cans with rubber bands, how to listen.

The city rebuilt itself around them. Ropes were strung along the main streets, knotted at each intersection in patterns anyone could learn by feel. Ration queues were organized by voice and rhythm. Marcus, blind since birth and once mocked in job interviews, handled food distribution entirely from memory, able to keep forty names and amounts in his head without writing a thing down.

Still, Teodora saw something that worried her. A few of the blind who had long been overlooked started taking a little too much pleasure in the reversal. During the third week, a man called Ferris, who had been sighted until the change, reached out for a ration and was sent away with a laugh. "Now you know," someone said to him. He just stood there, hand still extended.

That night Teodora went looking for him and found him on the curb, weeping softly.

"You're angry," she told him. "That's all right. I was angry for years."

"I just want this to end," he said under his breath.

"Maybe it won't." She sat down next to him. "But here's what nobody told me when I was six. It gets quieter. And then you begin to hear things."

For a long while he said nothing. Then he asked, "What do you hear?"

"Right now? A woman humming a couple of streets away. Someone repairing a pipe. A child laughing who still believes this is a game."

Ferris turned his face toward the sounds.

"Teach me," he said.

She took his hand, placed it on the rope line, and started.

Should Governments Spend on Space or on Earth's Problems?

A lot of people wonder whether governments should put money into exploring space. Some say that money ought to go toward fixing problems on Earth, such as hunger, disease and poverty. Both sides make good arguments, in my opinion, but I think governments should give a small share of their budget to space and use most of it for problems here at home.

To start, I can see why some people oppose spending on space. Every day, huge numbers of people around the world lack enough food or clean water. In poor countries, hospitals frequently do not have enough doctors or medicine. Seeing this, it is difficult to argue that sending a rocket to Mars matters more. Rockets cost a great deal, and a single launch may cost hundreds of millions of dollars. That money could pay for lots of schools or clinics.

On the other hand, exploring space brings us many advantages. Scientists working on space programs, for instance, have created many things we rely on daily. Satellites provide weather data, which lets farmers know when rain is coming and helps people prepare for major storms. The GPS in our phones also came from space technology. In addition, some medical equipment was developed from research done for astronauts. So the money does not simply vanish into the sky. It returns to Earth in a different form.

Space research can also help us understand our own planet. Viewing Earth from space lets scientists track how the climate is changing and how forests are shrinking. This information helps governments make wiser choices. Someday, space might even offer new resources or another home if serious problems strike Earth.

Space programs also create jobs for many people. Engineers, scientists, builders and teachers are all involved. Young people get excited about science because they dream of becoming astronauts. This benefits a country's future.

Even so, I believe governments need to be careful. Space usually takes up only a small portion of total government spending, so it is not the main reason Earth's problems remain unsolved. Still, if many people in a country have no home or food, those needs should be addressed first. A good government should take care of its citizens before it turns its eyes to the stars.

To conclude, I believe space exploration is valuable and should not be ended entirely. However, governments should devote most of their money to problems on Earth and keep space budgets modest and wise. That way, we can look after today while also getting ready for tomorrow.
