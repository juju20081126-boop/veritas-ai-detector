Being lost in the African wilderness depends more on sound judgment than heroics. Follow these steps in roughly this sequence.

### 1. Pause and evaluate

Fear causes more deaths than animals. Rest, drink some water if you have it, and look at your circumstances. What do you have with you? Does anybody know where you are? How long until someone notices you're gone? Your survival depends first on thinking clearly.

### 2. Remain near your vehicle if you have one

If you have a car or plane, **stay with it.** It provides shade and is easy for rescuers to spot. Vehicles hold useful items: reflective surfaces for signaling, cloth to shield from sun, materials that make thick smoke when burned. The water in the cooling system might work in an emergency, but only drink it if there's no antifreeze. Most people who perish in these situations left their vehicles. Leave only when you're confident no help is coming and you know where to go.

### 3. Manage heat and prevent dehydration

Extreme dryness poses the biggest threat. People can die within a couple of days without water, quicker in intense heat.

- **Minimize water loss instead of rationing it.** Drink what your body needs, but decrease sweat. Rest during peak heat, roughly 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. Keep clothes on to shield from sun, cover your head, and keep your mouth closed.
- **Travel during cool times: dawn, dusk, or after dark,** and only if absolutely necessary.
- **Locating water:**
  - Look for vegetation, clusters of trees such as tall figs, or reed patches. These signal water below ground.
  - Dig in dried riverbeds, especially on the **inside** of a bend, where moisture accumulates beneath the surface.
  - Notice animals. Paths where game trails **converge** often lead to water. Doves and sandgrouse fly straight toward water at dawn and dusk. Swarms of insects often indicate water is close.
  - Collect dew using fabric, or fashion a solar bag with a clear plastic covering over wet plants.
- **Purify water if feasible** by heating it. If the choice is between contaminated water and dying of thirst, drink it. You can recover from illness after rescue. Severe dehydration is fatal.
- **Never drink urine, liquor, or ocean water,** and consume minimally if water is scarce, since your body uses water to digest food.

### 4. Create shelter and fire

- **Shelter:** You need shade in daylight and warmth at night, as desert and savanna get cold when the sun goes down. A cloth, branches, or stone overhang will work. Remove stones and watch for scorpions or snakes.
- **Poor camping spots:** Not on animal paths, not in empty riverbeds (storms miles away can flood them suddenly), and not at water's edge where animals and predators gather, especially after dark.
- **Fire:** Warmth, clean water, visibility to rescuers, and animal deterrent. Gather dry kindling **before** nightfall. Thorn wood can be piled into a barrier like the *boma* herders build.

### 5. Get noticed

- Use **three** signals: three fires, three rock piles, three horn sounds. Three means SOS.
- In daytime, toss dried plants or plastic onto fire to make dark smoke.
- Shine a mirror, phone screen, or shiny object toward aircraft or distant cars.
- Make big signals that contrast with surroundings, like "SOS" or "X" from stones or branches.
- Check your phone from high places when possible. A single signal bar might be enough for an emergency call or text.

### 6. Know the dangerous animals

Most creatures avoid humans, but follow these rules:

- **Lions and leopards:** **Don't run.** This triggers a chase. Straighten up, make yourself look large, face the animal, yell, and back away calmly.
- **Elephants:** Keep distance. Flared ears and raised head might be bluffing, but silence with ears back means a real attack. Get a large tree or rock between you and the elephant.
- **Hippos and crocodiles:** The deadliest. Stay far from rivers and lakes, especially at sunrise, sunset, and night.
- **Cape buffalo:** Aggressive, especially alone.
- **Snakes and scorpions:** Be careful with hand and foot placement. Shake out clothes and footwear before wearing. Step on top of obstacles rather than over them.
- **Mosquitoes:** Cover skin at nightfall to reduce bites and malaria danger.

### 7. If walking becomes necessary

Move during the cool hours and head straight toward rescue. Roads, paths, fences, electrical poles, and waterways (at a distance) usually lead to civilization. Look for signs of human settlement: herds, smoke, footpaths. Locals are usually your best bet for help, so be respectful and ask for assistance.

### 8. Hunger is the last priority

You can go weeks without food, so never eat unfamiliar plants, fungi, or berries, as many are poisonous. Termites and recognized safe options like baobab can provide some nutrition, but water, shelter, and rescue matter more.

### Most importantly

Getting ready is the most important survival tool. Before heading into wilderness, tell someone your path and expected return, bring extra water and equipment, and find an experienced local guide when possible.

```dockerfile
# 1. Start with a slim, official Node.js image
FROM node:20-alpine

# 2. Set the work directory inside the container
WORKDIR /usr/src/app

# 3. Copy package files first
COPY package*.json ./

# 4. Install dependencies for production only
RUN npm ci --omit=dev

# 5. Copy the rest of the application code
COPY . .

# 6. Set the app to production mode
ENV NODE_ENV=production

# 7. Specify which port the app runs on
EXPOSE 3000

# 8. Run with a standard user, not root
USER node

# 9. The startup command
CMD ["node", "server.js"]
```

## What's happening here

**`FROM node:20-alpine`**
Docker images stack on top of each other. This selects Node.js 20 on Alpine Linux, which is very compact (several dozen megabytes rather than gigabytes). Node and npm come preinstalled.

**`WORKDIR /usr/src/app`**
Creates the directory if needed and switches to it. All following steps work there. Keeps everything organized and prevents overwriting system files.

**`COPY package*.json ./`**
Copies `package.json` and `package-lock.json` into the image *before* application files. Why? **Caching by layers**: Docker caches each instruction, so if these files haven't changed, npm reinstall is skipped the next time. Saves build time when only source code changes.

**`RUN npm ci --omit=dev`**
Installs exactly what `package-lock.json` specifies. `npm ci` is better than `npm install` for consistency, and `--omit=dev` removes unnecessary tools like test runners.

**`COPY . .`**
Brings remaining application files in.

**`ENV NODE_ENV=production`**
Sets a variable. Frameworks check this to activate optimizations like caching and quieter error output.

**`EXPOSE 3000`**
Documents that the app listens on port 3000. This doesn't open the port; you must use `-p` when starting the container.

**`USER node`**
Node images provide a standard user called `node`. Using it instead of root limits damage if your app gets exploited.

**`CMD ["node", "server.js"]`**
The default command when the container starts. Replace `server.js` with your entry point. Running `node` directly instead of `npm start` ensures your app gets termination signals like `SIGTERM` properly.

## Add a `.dockerignore`

Create a `.dockerignore` file where the Dockerfile is:

```
node_modules
npm-debug.log
.git
.env
Dockerfile
.dockerignore
```

Excluding `node_modules` is important: local modules might be for your OS, but the image builds its own.

## Build and run

```bash
docker build -t my-node-app .
docker run -p 3000:3000 my-node-app
```

The first creates an image with tag `my-node-app`. The second launches a container and links port 3000 on your system to port 3000 in the container, so you can reach `http://localhost:3000`.

If your app has a compilation phase (TypeScript, bundling, etc.), use a multi-stage build: compile and install dependencies in the first stage, then copy just the compiled code and production dependencies into a smaller final image.
