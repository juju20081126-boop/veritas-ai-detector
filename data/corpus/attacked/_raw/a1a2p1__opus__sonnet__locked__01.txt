# Surviving in the African Bush

Survival experts rely on a guideline known as the "rule of threes": you can go roughly three minutes without air, three hours in harsh exposure, three days without water, and three weeks without food. In the African bush, heat and water are the urgent concerns, and the wildlife brings a danger of its own. Here is how I would set my priorities.

## 1. Stay calm and stop moving

The first error most people make is to panic and wander away. Sit down, take a few slow breaths, and check what you have with you: water, clothing, a phone, a lighter, a knife, a mirror, anything at all. Then think about how you ended up here. If people know your route and are expecting you back, **staying where you are is nearly always the better option**, since a stationary person near a vehicle or landmark is much easier to locate than one on the move. If your vehicle has broken down, stay with it. A car can be seen from the air, provides shade, and contains useful materials like mirrors, reflective surfaces, a battery, and oil for a smoky fire.

Leave only if you are sure nobody is searching for you, you know precisely where help is, and you carry enough water to get there.

## 2. Water comes first

In the bush heat you can become dangerously dehydrated in a day or two. Your priorities:

- **Ration sweat, not water.** Rest in the shade during the hottest part of the day, roughly 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., and if you have to move, do it only at dawn, at dusk, or by moonlight.
- **Keep your clothes on.** Loose, pale clothing, a hat, and long sleeves cut down on water loss and sun exposure.
- **Don't eat if you have no water.** Digesting food uses up water.
- **Look for natural sources.** Animals can guide you. Birds flying low toward one spot in the morning and evening, bees, and big herds often lead to water. Dry riverbeds may hold water just under the surface on the outside of bends. Dig a hole there and let the water seep in.
- **Collect dew.** Wrap absorbent cloth around your ankles and walk through the grass at dawn, then wring it out. You can also leave cloth or plastic out overnight.
- **Purify what you find.** Boil it if possible. If not, strain it through cloth. A dirty source still beats none when the alternative is collapse, but treat it as a last resort.

Never drink urine or blood. Both make dehydration worse.

## 3. Shelter and shade

During the day, sun and heat are the main threat, and nights can turn surprisingly cold, particularly on the high plateau and in the dry season.

- Create shade with a vehicle, a tree, a tarp, or a dense bush. Raise a cover about a foot above your body so air can move.
- Insulate yourself from the hot ground by sitting or lying on a pile of grass or branches.
- At night, add insulation with dry grass, leaves, and layers of clothing.
- Pick your spot with care. Don't camp right on game trails, at waterholes, or beneath trees with dead limbs. Stay out of riverbeds that could flash flood if it rains upstream.

## 4. Signal for rescue

Rescuers have to be able to see or hear you.

- **Fire.** Keep a small fire ready to light. Flames are visible from far off at night. During the day, add green leaves or something damp to make thick smoke.
- **Mirror or any shiny object.** A flash can be seen for miles, and you can sweep it along the horizon or toward aircraft.
- **Ground signals.** Make large, high-contrast shapes in an open area: three of anything (fires, rocks, piles of branches) is a recognized distress signal.
- **Whistle or phone.** Three blasts, repeated, is the universal distress signal. Check your phone for even a faint signal and send a text, because texts often get through when calls fail. Save the battery.

## 5. Deal with wildlife sensibly

Most animals would rather avoid you. The real danger comes from startling them or getting between a mother and her young.

- **Lions and leopards:** Don't run, since running sets off a chase. Stand tall, face the animal, talk firmly, and back away slowly. Keep a fire burning at night. Predators tend to be wary of flames.
- **Elephants and buffalo:** Give them a wide berth, stay downwind when you can, and avoid thick bush where you might stumble into them.
- **Hippos:** They cause many deaths. Keep away from the water's edge at night, when they graze on land, and never get between a hippo and the water.
- **Snakes and scorpions:** Watch where you put your hands and feet, shake out shoes and clothes, and use a stick to turn over rocks.
- **Hyenas and jackals:** Mostly scavengers, but they can get bold at night around a lone person. Make noise and keep a fire going.
- **Insects:** Mosquitoes and tsetse flies spread disease. Cover your skin and use smoke if possible.

If you have any food, keep it sealed and away from where you sleep.

## 6. Food is a lower priority

Since you can survive for weeks without food, don't take risks to get it, particularly when water is short. If you're stranded for many days, you can eat things you can identify with certainty. But in unfamiliar territory, **avoid unknown plants and mushrooms**, because poisoning would cost you water and strength. Cooked insects such as termites and grasshoppers are a safe, nutritious choice. Wild honey is rich in calories but fiercely guarded, so it is seldom worth the risk.

## 7. Staying healthy

- Look out for signs of heat illness: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, and sweating that stops. Cool down right away with shade, wet clothing, and rest.
- Look after your feet. Blisters and infections turn serious without treatment.
- Clean any cuts with the cleanest water you have and keep them covered.
- Keep your mind busy. Give yourself small jobs like gathering wood, checking signals, and rationing water on a schedule. Morale counts for a lot, and people with a plan usually outlast those who give up.

## If you must walk out

Move only if you have no choice. Travel at night or in the early morning, follow a drainage line or a road if you find one, and mark your path so rescuers can track it. Leave notes at your starting point with the date, time, and the direction you're heading. Pace yourself and rest frequently.

## The short version

Stay put, find shade, ration water, signal constantly, keep clear of wildlife, and keep a clear head. Most people rescued in the bush are found because they stayed visible and stayed in one place, not because they set off heroically on foot.

# Chakras and Spiritual Growth

In the traditions that gave rise to the idea, chakras are not an extra layered on top of spiritual practice. They are a map of it. The word is Sanskrit and means "wheel" or "disc." The concept shows up in Hindu and Buddhist tantric texts, in yoga philosophy, and in later esoteric writings. It describes energy centers running along the spine, typically seven in today's popular version. Each is linked to a dimension of human experience, and the idea is that working with them helps a person grow more whole, more aware, and more at peace.

## The seven centers and what they represent

- **Root (Muladhara):** Located at the base of the spine. It is connected with safety, survival, belonging, and feeling grounded. Spiritual growth here means developing a sense of stability so that fear doesn't drive your choices.
- **Sacral (Svadhisthana):** Found in the lower abdomen. It is connected with pleasure, creativity, and emotional flow. Growth here involves a healthy relationship with desire and feeling.
- **Solar plexus (Manipura):** Found in the upper abdomen. It is connected with willpower, confidence, and personal identity. Growth here means acting with purpose without slipping into ego.
- **Heart (Anahata):** In the middle of the chest. It is connected with love, compassion, and forgiveness. Many traditions see it as the bridge between the "lower" and "higher" centers.
- **Throat (Vishuddha):** It is connected with honest expression and communication. Growth here is about speaking and living in keeping with your values.
- **Third eye (Ajna):** Between the eyebrows. It is connected with intuition, insight, and clear perception.
- **Crown (Sahasrara):** At the top of the head. It is connected with transcendence, a sense of oneness, and connection to something larger than the self.

## How chakras are used as a path

The core idea is that spiritual development isn't a single big leap. It is a gradual process of noticing where you are stuck and then working on that. If you struggle with trust and anxiety, a practitioner might say your root is unsettled. If you have trouble saying what you really think, they might point to the throat. The chakra system gives people a vocabulary for self-inquiry, turning a vague sense that "something is off" into a more specific question to explore.

Practices linked to chakras include meditation and visualization, breathwork (pranayama), mantra and sound, particular yoga postures, and sometimes color or crystals. A meditation might ask you to rest your attention on one center, picture it as a spinning light, and notice whatever sensations or emotions arise. Over time, the goal is to shift from reactivity toward balance.

In classical yoga and tantra, the bigger aim is the rising of kundalini, a dormant energy said to rest at the base of the spine. As it climbs through the centers, it is thought to dissolve limiting patterns until awareness reaches the crown and the feeling of separateness fades. This is the most ambitious form of the idea. Most people today use a gentler version, as a framework for emotional and personal reflection.

## Where the benefit really comes from

It's worth being honest about the evidence. Nothing in science supports chakras as literal energy structures in the body, and researchers have not found them anatomically. What research does back up is the practices themselves. Meditation, slow breathing, and mindful movement have documented effects on stress, attention, and emotional regulation. Many people find that the chakra framework gives these practices structure and meaning, and that structure keeps them returning.

So the role of chakras in spiritual growth is partly practical and partly symbolic. They provide:

1. **A map for self-reflection.** Seven themes cover a great deal: security, creativity, confidence, love, expression, insight, and meaning.
2. **A reason to stay consistent.** Moving through the centers creates a sense of progress, which supports a regular practice.
3. **A shared language.** Teachers and students can discuss inner experience in common terms.
4. **A holistic view.** The system treats body, emotion, and spirit as linked rather than separate.

## A few cautions

Chakra work shouldn't take the place of medical or psychological care. If someone is coping with depression, trauma, or physical illness, "unblocking a chakra" is no substitute for professional help. It's also worth keeping in mind that the modern seven-chakra model, with its rainbow colors, is mostly a twentieth-century Western development. Older traditions describe different numbers of centers with different meanings. Holding the framework lightly, as a tool rather than a literal truth, tends to serve people better.

Seen that way, chakras can be a helpful lens. They encourage you to slow down, pay attention to your inner life, and take seriously the idea that growth involves the whole person.

In the months that followed, Odile began to notice it everywhere. Her neighbor Pell was a cello, all long, patient silences interrupted by a sudden, rich vibration whenever someone said something kind. The bus driver was a brass section, loud and slightly sharp but warm underneath. Her nine-year-old niece was a xylophone being hit by a dozen mallets at once, gloriously out of tune with herself and not bothered in the least.

And Odile herself? She had never thought to wonder. She tuned other people's pianos and left her own life with a sticky pedal and a stuck key in the upper register.

"Then come and hear everything right, just this once. Sit beside someone who's only a little out of tune."

**What he did**

- **Special relativity (1905):** He demonstrated that space and time aren't fixed but depend on how you're moving, and that nothing can go faster than light. One consequence of the theory is E = mc², which connects mass and energy and explains why nuclear reactions release so much power.

Most ordinary townspeople fetched water home themselves in jugs and buckets, while in bigger towns professional water carriers sold it. Cisterns were used to collect rainwater. The quality of water differed widely. Since tanneries, butchers and sewage often fouled the rivers, it was better to take water from springs or from upstream. With water sometimes unsafe to drink, people often turned to ale or weak beer, which was safer thanks to boiling and fermentation.

Is Using Animals in Scientific Research Ethical?

A lot of people wonder whether it is right to use animals for scientific research. To me, this is a hard question, since both sides have strong arguments. In this essay I will give my opinion.

To begin with, animal research has been very helpful to humans. Before doctors gave many medicines and vaccines to people, those treatments were first tested on animals. The polio vaccine and many cancer drugs, for instance, were created with the help of animals such as mice and monkeys. Many people alive today would have died without this research. Some believe that using animals is acceptable when a scientist can save thousands of human lives.

On the other hand, animals experience pain and fear in the same way we do. A laboratory mouse has no way to refuse or to walk away. Certain experiments cause animals great suffering, which I find very sad. As living creatures, animals have the right to live without being harmed. Some people argue that if we treat our pets kindly, we should also be kind to animals in labs.

A further issue is that animals and humans are different. A drug may work well in a mouse but fail in a human, or even turn out to be dangerous. This means the results are not always useful, so some animals suffer for no reason, which is unfair.

Luckily, scientists now have alternatives. They can test new drugs with computer models or with human cells grown in the lab. These approaches are often cheaper and harm no animals. In many countries, rules also require scientists to use the smallest possible number of animals and to look after them well. I believe these rules matter a great deal.

My view is that animal research can be ethical only in certain cases. When the research is truly needed to cure serious illnesses and there is no alternative, it can be acceptable. Even then, scientists should always try to reduce the animals' pain and use new technology to replace them whenever they can. We must remember that animals are living beings as well.

To conclude, I don't believe the answer is a simple yes or no. Science matters for our future, but so does kindness. By being careful and responsible, we can protect both human health and animal life.

We calculate the strange quark helicity distribution in the proton within chiral effective theory. Data from polarized deep inelastic scattering have long hinted at a sizable, negative strange quark contribution to the proton spin, whereas semi-inclusive measurements suggest a much smaller value, leaving the issue unresolved. We examine the contribution from the pion-baryon and kaon-hyperon fluctuations of the nucleon, in which a strange quark and antiquark arise via the kaon cloud. Working with the chiral effective Lagrangian including octet and decuplet intermediate states, we compute the leading nonanalytic contributions to the strange helicity and their dependence on the ultraviolet regulator.
