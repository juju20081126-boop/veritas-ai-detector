The question "how many squares are on a chessboard?" has two answers. Count only the 64 small light and dark cells and you get **64**. Count every square you could outline on the board, large ones built from smaller cells included, and the figure rises to **204**.

## Squares of every size

Beyond the 64 single cells, the 8 × 8 grid hides squares measuring 2 × 2, 3 × 3, and so on, all the way to the whole board, which counts as a single 8 × 8 square. What matters is how many places each size can sit.

Start with 2 × 2. Its upper-left corner may fall in any of the first 7 columns and any of the first 7 rows, giving 7 × 7 = 49 squares. The same reasoning shows that a k × k square has (9 − k)² possible positions:

| Square size | How many |
|---|---|
| 1 × 1 | 8² = 64 |
| 2 × 2 | 7² = 49 |
| 3 × 3 | 6² = 36 |
| 4 × 4 | 5² = 25 |
| 5 × 5 | 4² = 16 |
| 6 × 6 | 3² = 9 |
| 7 × 7 | 2² = 4 |
| 8 × 8 | 1² = 1 |
| **Total** | **204** |

## A formula for any board

Adding these up means summing the first eight perfect squares, and a standard identity covers that sum for the first n squares:

1² + 2² + 3² + ... + n² = n(n + 1)(2n + 1) / 6

Setting n = 8 gives (8 × 9 × 17) / 6 = 1,224 / 6 = 204, which agrees with the table. Any square grid works the same way; a 3 × 3 tic-tac-toe grid holds 9 + 4 + 1 = 14 squares.

## Extending the puzzle to rectangles

Some versions ask about rectangles too. A rectangle is fixed by choosing two of the board's 9 horizontal lines and two of its 9 vertical lines. Each pair can be chosen in C(9, 2) = 36 ways, so there are 36 × 36 = **1,296** rectangles in all, and 204 of them are squares.

## Summary

- **64** single squares
- **204** squares of any size
- **1,296** rectangles of any size, squares among them

This is a classic trick question because most people answer 64 and stop. A full answer states which reading you mean and shows the sum behind 204.

"He told me the ocean would take everything in the end," the man said. "I suppose I wanted to prove him right on my own timetable."

Dr. Vance made a note. She never wrote the word *villain* in her files. She had given up the term early in her career, once she realized it named an outcome rather than a person. Her clients had done terrible things, and some were still capable of more. Her job was not to absolve them. It was to understand what had brought them to the edge, and whether any of them could still be talked into stepping back.

At eleven, a young woman who called herself the Archivist turned up twelve minutes late and apologized four times. She had wiped the memories of a whole town in pursuit of what she called a cleaner history. In session she was meticulous, anxious, and afraid of being forgotten herself. Dr. Vance noted, not for the first time, that grand ambitions often hid very small and very old fears.

"Do you think I can change?" the Archivist asked.

"I think you're asking," Dr. Vance answered, "and that is not nothing."

Her afternoon client was the hardest. He had no particular grievance, no wounded childhood he would admit to, and no stated goal beyond what he called "the elegance of collapse." Therapy amused him, and he treated every session as a chess game in which he was several moves ahead.

"You know what I am," he said. "Why keep going?"

She did not think of herself as brave. She thought of herself as a professional who had taken on an unusual caseload. Everyone, she had come to believe, was the hero of their own story, however warped. Her task was to sit with that story long enough to find the first wrong turn.

Now and then, she found it. Now and then, the client turned back.

Those were the days she remembered, and the reason she kept the office on the fourteenth floor.

# Maine Wild Food Survival Guide

## The Golden Rules

1. **Do not eat anything unless you are completely certain what it is.** A single error with a mushroom or a wild carrot lookalike can be fatal.
2. **Fat and protein count for more than greens.** Eating only lean meat, rabbit especially, brings on "rabbit starvation." Look for fat: beaver tail, nuts, fish, bone marrow.
3. **Boil every drop of water.** Giardia is present even in the clearest brook.
4. **Plan by season.** Maine is generous from May through October and stingy afterward. Put food away all summer.

## Plants and Greens

**Cattail** (marshes, pond edges) is the most useful single plant in Maine. Peeled spring shoots taste like cucumber. Green flower heads can be boiled and eaten like corn. The rhizomes provide starch all year, even beneath ice.

**Fiddleheads** (ostrich fern, May) grow on river floodplains. Pick only ones with a smooth stem and a deep U-shaped groove along the inner side, like celery. Always boil them 10 minutes or more, because raw ones make you sick.

**Dandelion, chickweed, lamb's quarters, plantain, purslane, and wood sorrel** are common, safe, and loaded with vitamins. Dandelion roots can be roasted in the fall.

**Seaweed** on the coast is plentiful and nourishing: dulse, kelp, Irish moss, and rockweed. Glasswort (sea beans) grows in salt marshes and tastes crisp and salty.

**Pine and spruce:** in an emergency the inner bark (cambium) can be eaten, and tea made from needles supplies vitamin C through winter. Stay away from yew, which has flat, soft needles and red berries and is deadly.

## Fruits and Nuts

- **Lowbush blueberries** (July to August) blanket barrens and burned ground. Sun-dry them for winter.
- **Wild strawberries** (June), **raspberries and blackberries** (July to August)
- **Cranberries** grow in bogs (September to October) and keep for weeks.
- **Chokecherries and pin cherries:** eat the flesh and spit out the pits, which contain cyanide compounds.
- **Rose hips** hang on into winter and are full of vitamin C.
- **Beechnuts, hazelnuts, and butternuts** are rich in fat.
- **Acorns** are abundant but bitter. Crack them, then soak or boil them in several changes of water until the bitterness is gone. Grind them into flour.

## Fungi

Learn a few species, and learn them flawlessly.

- **Chanterelles** (summer, hardwoods): golden, with blunt false ridges running down the stem. Jack-o'-lantern mushrooms are orange, grow in clusters on wood, and have true sharp gills.
- **Chicken of the woods:** bright orange-yellow shelves on dead oak. You can't mistake it.
- **Oyster mushrooms:** fan-shaped, on dead hardwood, found nearly all year.
- **Hen of the woods:** big gray clusters at the foot of oaks in fall.
- **Giant puffballs:** cut them from top to bottom. The inside must be pure white with no outline of a mushroom shape, since that would indicate a deadly Amanita button.
- **Morels** (spring): completely hollow inside. False morels are cottony or chambered within.

Never eat a white-gilled mushroom. The destroying angel occurs throughout Maine.

## Animals

**Big game:** white-tailed deer and moose yield hundreds of pounds of meat. Use all of it: hide, sinew, bone, and fat.

**Small game:** snowshoe hares and squirrels are easy to snare along trails. Ruffed grouse can often be knocked down with a thrown stick. Porcupines are slow and can be killed with a club, making them a classic emergency food. Beavers are fatty and live in almost every watershed.

**Wild turkeys** are common in southern and central Maine.

**Fish:** brook trout fill the cold streams. In spring, alewives and smelt swim upriver in enormous numbers and can be scooped out by hand or with a net. Ice fishing keeps you fed through winter.

**Shellfish:** soft-shell clams on mudflats, blue mussels on rocks, and periwinkles in tide pools. Lobsters can be caught in shallow water with simple traps. Watch for red tide: in warm months shellfish may carry paralytic toxins. If you eat a small amount and your lips tingle, stop right away.

**Insects and grubs** are dependable protein when nothing else is available. Cook them first.

## Deadly Plants to Know

- **Water hemlock:** found in wet places, with white umbrella-shaped flowers, purple-streaked stems, and a chambered root. It is the most poisonous plant in North America.
- **Poison hemlock:** looks similar, but smells musty and has purple blotches.
- **Baneberry:** white or red berries on stalks. Poisonous.
- **Any white umbrella-flowered plant** (carrot family): avoid completely unless you are an expert.

## Seasonal Plan

- **Spring:** fiddleheads, shoots, fish runs, morels
- **Summer:** berries, greens, shellfish, mushrooms
- **Fall:** nuts, roots, cranberries, hunting, smoking and drying meat
- **Winter:** stored food, ice fishing, snaring, cattail roots, needle tea

Preserve all you can. In Maine, winter determines who survives.

It went on like that for two hours. Nobody said it out loud. Everybody circled it. Uncle Ray told a story about a friend's son who "found his passion" after "a few false starts." My aunt remarked, out of nowhere, that "plenty of successful people changed direction." My grandfather, eighty-nine and mostly deaf, asked loudly, "Is this the one who flunked out?" and the room fell silent until Carol announced that the rolls were ready.

I did not flunk out. I left. There is a difference, though I doubted anyone at that table could hear it.

When we finally sat down to eat, my mother caught my eye. She looked wretched. I realized she had not been gossiping, or not only gossiping. She had been asking for help. She had asked everyone what to say to me, because she did not know.

I set down my fork.

"Okay," I said. "I quit law school."

The silence that followed was the purest I had ever heard in that house.

"I know you all know," I continued. "I would rather just talk about it than have everyone tiptoe around me all night. I wasn't happy. I was good at it, but I wasn't happy. Right now I'm working at a woodshop in Easton. I'm building furniture. I'm learning. And I'm fine."

No one moved. Then my grandfather, who for once had caught every word, said, "Furniture?"

"Furniture," I said.

He nodded slowly. "My father built furniture. Best man I ever knew."

Just like that, the room let out its breath. Uncle Ray asked which kind of wood I liked best. Megan confessed she hated her firm. My aunt asked whether I could fix her wobbly dining chair, the one she had been threatening to toss for ten years.

My mother said nothing. She simply reached under the table and squeezed my hand.

The glances lasted the rest of the night, but they had changed. They were no longer sidelong; they were curious. The smiles were no longer rehearsed; they were real.

On my way out, Aunt Carol gave me a bag of leftovers and the wobbly chair.

"No pressure," she said.

I laughed, and for the first time all evening, so did she.

Obama fought hard for fast-track authority (formally, Trade Promotion Authority) on the Trans-Pacific Partnership, and several linked reasons explain why.

**Making the deal possible.** Under fast-track, Congress votes yes or no on a trade agreement and cannot amend it. Other countries, Japan above all, were unwilling to make their last concessions if Congress could rewrite the terms afterward. Negotiators believed that without TPA the TPP would probably fall apart.

**Geopolitics and China.** The administration presented TPP as central to its "pivot to Asia." Obama argued that if the U.S. did not help write the trade rules for the Asia-Pacific, China would write them, on terms worse for American workers and businesses.

**Economic aims.** The agreement would lower tariffs on U.S. exports, set standards for intellectual property and digital trade, and contain labor and environmental provisions stronger than those in earlier deals such as NAFTA.

**Legacy.** TPP was a signature initiative of his second term.

The effort stood out because most of the opposition came from Obama's own party, so he teamed up with Republican leaders to pass TPA in 2015.

At the interface between the band insulators LaAlO₃ and SrTiO₃ sits a two-dimensional electron system with strikingly varied behavior: gate-tunable superconductivity, hints of magnetism, strong spin–orbit coupling and unusual magnetotransport. Whether these effects have a common microscopic origin has been an open question. We report systematic magnetotransport measurements, taken as a function of gate-controlled carrier density, on several interfaces that differ in LaAlO₃ thickness and electron mobility. Every sample shows a critical density near 1.7 × 10¹³ cm⁻² where transport switches abruptly from single-carrier to multi-carrier behavior. This density is universal and does not depend on layer thickness or mobility. The nature of the transition, along with its agreement with spectroscopic data, marks it as a Lifshitz transition, at which electrons start filling the heavier dxz/dyz bands in addition to the light dxy band. We also show that strong spin–orbit interaction and anomalous magnetoresistance set in at this same density. Together, these findings suggest that filling of orbital bands is a unifying principle behind the emergent physics of this oxide interface.
