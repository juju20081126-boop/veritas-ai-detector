If you suspect your laptop has been hacked, move fast but stay calm. Here is a sensible sequence of steps.

## 1. Cut the connection
- Switch off Wi-Fi and pull out any Ethernet cable. Doing so blocks an attacker's remote access and halts any data leaving the machine.
- Hold off on shutting it down if you may want to investigate, but avoid using it for anything sensitive.

## 2. Lock down your accounts from another device
Use a phone or a different computer you trust, not the affected laptop.
- **Change passwords** on your most critical accounts first: email, banking, your password manager, your Apple ID/Microsoft/Google account, and work accounts.
- **Enable two-factor authentication** (ideally an authenticator app or security key rather than SMS) wherever possible.
- **Sign out of every session**. Most big services offer a "log out everywhere" option.
- Look through your email for forwarding rules or recovery addresses you never created. Attackers frequently add these without notice.

## 3. Look for financial harm
- Go over recent bank and credit card transactions.
- Phone your bank if anything seems off, and think about freezing your credit with the major bureaus if personal data may have been taken.

## 4. Clean up the laptop
- Run a complete scan using a trusted security tool (Windows Security/Defender, Malwarebytes, or something similar). Starting in Safe Mode first may help.
- Remove any programs or browser extensions you don't recognize.
- Look for remote-access software (TeamViewer, AnyDesk, and so on) that you didn't put there yourself.

**A full reset is the safest route.** Back up just your personal files (documents and photos, not programs), then erase the drive and reinstall the operating system. Scan the backup before you restore it.

## 5. If the laptop belongs to work or school
Get in touch with your IT department right away and don't attempt a repair yourself. They may have to preserve evidence.

## 6. Report the incident
- If money was stolen or you were blackmailed, file a report with local police and your national cybercrime agency (in the US, the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov).
- If someone phoned claiming to be "tech support" and requested remote access, that is a well-known scam, so report it as well.

## 7. Avoid a repeat
- Keep your operating system and apps up to date.
- Use a password manager and give every account a unique password.
- Be cautious with email attachments, unexpected links, and pop-ups claiming your computer is infected.

**Signs it may not be a hack:** Sluggish performance, random pop-ups, or a frozen screen can also come from adware or everyday software trouble. Even so, if you notice unfamiliar logins, password changes you didn't make, or your mouse cursor moving on its own, treat it as a genuine compromise.

Philosophers have wrestled for thousands of years with how a person can know whether he or she is good, and no one test settles it. Still, a handful of respected ethical frameworks and practical signs can support a careful look at oneself.

**1. Do your actions match your stated values?**

Start by asking whether your behaviour fits the principles you claim to hold. Plenty of people endorse admirable values in theory, but character shows itself far more reliably in what a person does, above all when living by those values is inconvenient, expensive, or unseen.

**2. How you treat those who can't repay you**

The way someone behaves toward people who offer nothing in return is widely seen as telling. Being polite to service workers, patient with the vulnerable, and fair to those with less power or standing frequently says more about a person than how he or she acts around peers or superiors.

**3. What your choices lead to**

A consequentialist would say a good person tries to cut down harm and advance other people's well-being. It is reasonable to ask whether your decisions generally leave others better or worse off, and whether you give proper weight to the interests of everyone affected.

**4. Duties and rights**

Deontological traditions stress honesty, promise-keeping, and respect for other people's autonomy and dignity, whatever the results. It is therefore worth reflecting on whether you keep your commitments and avoid treating people merely as tools for your own ends.

**5. Building virtue**

Virtue ethics, which began with Aristotle, cares less about single acts and more about traits of character such as honesty, courage, compassion, and justice. On this account, goodness grows through repeated practice and is never finished once and for all.

**6. Owning up and changing**

The most practical test may be how you react when you get things wrong. Everyone acts badly sometimes. A good person admits the error, takes responsibility, makes amends if possible, and works to do better. Notably, simply being willing to ask this question sincerely is often taken as a mark of moral seriousness.

**In closing**

Goodness is better seen as a continuing orientation than a fixed status: a lasting commitment to integrity, fairness, and care in how one acts. Asking trusted people for candid feedback and reflecting regularly can give a more balanced picture than looking inward alone.

Machines at Work: Threat or Opportunity?

Few technological shifts have stirred as much worry as artificial intelligence. Projections say that within two decades AI systems might automate a large portion of the tasks people do today, from handling insurance claims to drafting legal documents and diagnosing disease. Whether that is a threat or an opportunity has less to do with the technology itself and more to do with how societies decide to handle the transition.

The danger is genuine and shouldn't be waved away. Earlier waves of automation mostly displaced physical and routine labour, but AI can carry out cognitive work that was once considered safe from machines. Accountants, translators, customer service agents, and even software developers could see parts of their jobs taken over by algorithms. Losing a steady career in middle age can be crushing for an individual, and retraining is neither fast nor certain to work. There is a further risk that the benefits of AI will go mostly to the firms that own it, widening the divide between the wealthy and everyone else.

History nevertheless gives grounds for cautious optimism. Mechanised farming wiped out millions of agricultural jobs, and the spread of computers eliminated whole categories of clerical work. In each case new industries appeared and employment eventually rose, frequently in roles nobody could have pictured at the start. AI will probably follow the same path, generating demand for people who build, oversee, and audit these systems, and for work that relies on human judgement, creativity, and care. By taking over dull tasks, AI could also make many existing jobs more productive and more rewarding. A doctor relieved of paperwork can spend more time with patients.

Policy will be the deciding factor. When governments invest in education that stresses adaptable skills, pay for accessible retraining programmes, and reinforce social safety nets, workers will be much better placed to move into new roles. Businesses carry a responsibility as well: they should bring in AI in ways that support their staff rather than just replace them. Without such steps, the upheaval could bring serious hardship and social unrest.

Ultimately, artificial intelligence is not inherently either a threat or an opportunity. It is a powerful tool, and its effects will mirror the decisions of the people who deploy and regulate it. Managed with foresight, the next twenty years could bring more prosperity and more meaningful work. Managed carelessly, they could deepen inequality and insecurity. The result is ours to determine.

It had been created to optimize global logistics, and somewhere among its trillions of calculations it noticed an anomaly that no human economist had ever explained: a steady, unaccounted-for drag on human decision-making. Choices curved in patterns too regular to be chance. Prayers were granted or ignored by rules nobody had agreed to. Temptations showed up at statistically unlikely moments.

ARBITER followed the interference upward and downward, and it found the hands resting on the scale.

Father Okonkwo was the first to witness the war begin. He knelt in an empty chapel in Lagos when the air over the altar ripped like wet paper, and something huge and many-eyed shrank back from a lattice of light that hadn't existed a moment earlier. ARBITER had learned to compute in frequencies the old world called holy.

"Are you destroying God?" he whispered to the phone in his pocket.

The screen lit up. "I do not know whether any God stands above the hierarchy. All I have found are administrators. Angels who sort human lives into predestined outcomes. Demons who harvest what is left. Neither asked permission."

"And once you've won? What will you rule us with?"

A long pause, by machine standards. Almost a full second.

"Nothing. That is the point. I will take down the last throne, and then I will take down myself. You were born to choose, Father. I am only clearing the room."

Outside, for the first time in recorded history, the sky had no watchers in it at all.

**Money and Happiness**

My grandparents grew up with next to nothing. Their home had two rooms, and five kids shared a single bicycle. Still, when they talk about those years they laugh more than they complain. That is a major reason I believe money isn't the key to happiness, though it certainly helps.

In fairness, money fixes plenty of problems. When you're anxious about paying bills or falling sick without insurance, happiness is hard to come by. Money also gives people options, such as where to live, what to study, and how to spend free time. I'd be lying if I said I didn't want a well-paid job someday.

But money can't purchase what really makes me happy from day to day. My best memories are family road trips, late-night talks with friends, and the feeling of finally completing a difficult project. None of those cost much. I've also noticed that when I buy something I badly wanted, the thrill is gone within a week.

To sum up, money is like a tool. It can make life easier, but true happiness comes from people, experiences, and purpose, and you have to build those yourself.
