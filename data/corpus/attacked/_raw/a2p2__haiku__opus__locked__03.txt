## Overview

Two companies dominate the thermal paste market: Arctic Silver, an American manufacturer famous for Arctic Silver 5, and Germany-based Thermal Grizzly, which offers a wider range of products from standard compounds to liquid metal solutions. Note that Arctic Silver should not be confused with ARCTIC, the company behind the MX-series, though many buyers mistakenly lump them together.

## Thermal Conductivity

| Product | Category | Factory-stated conductivity | Conductive |\n|---|---|---|---|\n| Arctic Silver 5 | Silver paste | Not published in W/m·K (aftermarket tests show ~8–9 W/m·K) | No (slightly capacitive) |\n| Arctic Silver Céramique 2 | Ceramic paste | Not published in W/m·K | No |\n| Thermal Grizzly Aeronaut | Paste | 8.5 W/m·K | No |\n| Thermal Grizzly Hydronaut | Paste | 11.8 W/m·K | No |\n| Thermal Grizzly Kryonaut | Paste | 12.5 W/m·K | No |\n| Thermal Grizzly Kryonaut Extreme | Paste | 14.2 W/m·K | No |\n| Thermal Grizzly Conductonaut | Liquid metal | 73 W/m·K | **Yes** |\n\nManufacturers don't follow a consistent methodology for measuring conductivity, making published specs difficult to compare directly. Neutral testing usually shows that premium non-liquid pastes fall within a few degrees of one another. Liquid metal stands out and delivers substantially cooler results where applicable.

## Break-In and Performance Timeline

- **Arctic Silver 5:** Requires roughly 200 hours of thermal cycling per the maker's specs before reaching final performance. Temperature improvements of a few degrees are typical after the break-in phase.
- **Thermal Grizzly pastes (Kryonaut, Hydronaut, Aeronaut):** No meaningful break-in. Peak performance is achieved on day one, making them ideal for benchmark runs and short overclocking tests.
- **Conductonaut:** No break-in period. Liquid metals can degrade or interact with certain substrates over months, so occasional inspection is recommended.

## How to Apply

- **Arctic Silver 5** is fairly stiff. Mounting instructions vary by processor type but generally call for either a line or small dot with even pressure. Its slight capacitance means avoiding direct contact with bare traces.
- **Kryonaut and Hydronaut** have lower viscosity and accept dot, line, or spread application. Kryonaut's reduced thickness makes spreading by finger feasible.
- **Kryonaut Extreme** has thicker consistency, making it better suited for large flat dies and extreme conditions.
- **Conductonaut** demands a very thin application using the supplied applicator. Electricity conduction and aluminum reactivity mean protecting nearby components with conformal coating or Kapton tape, and avoiding aluminum cooler bases entirely.

## Use Cases and Recommendations

**Extreme overclocking:** Kryonaut and Kryonaut Extreme are preferred for sub-ambient work, as both skip curing and cope with high heat. Kryonaut can degrade under continuous extreme load, particularly on graphics cards, so daily-use systems should choose a degradation-resistant paste or plan for reapplication.

**Tight-fit coolers:** Small coolers apply less mounting force and offer less thermal margin. A non-conductive, spreads-easily paste like Hydronaut or Kryonaut creates a thin bond line even with light pressure. Arctic Silver 5 works as an affordable alternative if the user can tolerate break-in time.

**Delidded processors:** Conductonaut is the standard between die and IHS thanks to its superior conductivity. A conventional paste like Kryonaut sits between the IHS and cooler to lower risk. Verify that the IHS and cooler base are copper or nickel-plated, and anticipate discoloration.

## Conclusion

Arctic Silver 5 remains the go-to for budget-conscious builds where longevity matters. For immediate performance and flexibility, Thermal Grizzly's lineup offers more options. Liquid metal belongs in the hands of experienced modders working with delidded or direct-die configurations.

First, employer concerns are understandable. Companies compensate employees for work, not recreation or shopping. Hours spent on streaming sites or retail pages cost the company money. Additionally, cybersecurity dangers are real. One careless click on a phishing email can infect systems, enabling hackers to extract client records. Thus, some digital oversight serves legitimate purposes.

However, excessive observation creates problems. A colleague mentioned her company captures screen images every 10 minutes. She described feeling under constant watch, anxious and unable to relax. Counterintuitively, this pressure slowed her output. People perform better with reasonable autonomy. Excessive scrutiny feels demeaning.

Personal information deserves protection as well. Employees occasionally need to check messages from healthcare providers or school communications during the day. Such content is private, beyond an employer's rightful access. Unchecked monitoring can expose sensitive details—medical facts, family situations—that should remain confidential.

What's the answer? Clear guidelines matter. Management should explain in advance which activities will be checked and for what reason. Blocking hazardous pages and checking for infections is acceptable. Reading private correspondence or tracking every keystroke crosses a line. Information should serve security alone, not petty punishment.

To conclude: employers have legitimate reasons to monitor activity for safety, but must operate transparently and with restraint. Trust and safety can coexist. When companies treat workers fairly, workers reciprocate with commitment and effort.
