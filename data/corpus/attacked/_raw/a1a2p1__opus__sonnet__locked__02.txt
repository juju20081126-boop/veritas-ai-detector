"All work and no play makes Jack a dull boy" is a proverb cautioning that a person who only ever works will grow boring, exhausted and, in the end, less effective. The saying has existed in English for hundreds of years, and versions of it show up in proverb collections dating back to the 1600s.

**The thinking behind it**

Rest and play are not the enemy of productivity. They are one of the things that make it possible. Someone who works nonstop gets ground down: focus wavers, creativity runs dry, and stress builds. **Why the word "dull"?**

The word "dull" carries two meanings here. Jack turns dull in that he becomes uninteresting, with no topic of conversation besides his job. He is also dull the way a blunt knife is, with a mind that has lost its edge. Each meaning implies that relentless effort with no variety wears people out.

**What the research says**

Contemporary psychology backs up the proverb. Research on burnout finds that people who seldom take breaks are less motivated and make more errors. Rest lets the brain consolidate memories and form fresh connections, which explains why good ideas so often come during a shower or a walk. Play also benefits mental health, social relationships and problem-solving.

**A cultural echo**

The line appears famously in Stephen King's novel *The Shining* and in Stanley Kubrick's film adaptation, where Jack Torrance types it repeatedly across page after page. There it turns into an eerie symbol of isolation and obsession, precisely what the proverb cautions against.

**The lesson**

The real message is balance. Working hard is important, but a life with no enjoyment is both poorer and less productive. Set aside time for rest and play, and your work will usually improve as well.

Through the photovoltaic effect, solar panels turn sunlight straight into electricity. The majority of panels are made from silicon solar cells, thin wafers of silicon that have been treated so that they contain an electric field.

**What happens inside a cell**

Silicon is classed as a semiconductor. When producing a solar cell, manufacturers dope two layers in different ways. Phosphorus is added to the upper layer, supplying extra electrons and making that layer n-type. Boron is added to the lower layer, producing electron gaps known as holes and making that layer p-type. At the boundary between them a p-n junction forms, which sets up a built-in electric field.

**How current is produced**

If a sufficiently energetic photon hits the silicon, it frees an electron from its atom and leaves a hole in its place, forming an electron-hole pair. The junction's electric field drives the electrons toward the n-type side and the holes toward the p-type side, pulling the charges apart before they have a chance to recombine.

These electrons are gathered by metal contacts on the cell's front and back. Once an external circuit links the contacts, the electrons travel through it as direct current, powering devices along the way and then returning to the opposite side of the cell.

**From a single cell to a full system**

Each cell generates only around 0.5 volts, so a panel contains dozens of them wired in series, and panels are in turn joined into arrays. Since homes run on alternating current, an inverter changes the DC output into AC. Textured surfaces and anti-reflective coatings help the cell capture more light.

A typical panel turns about 18 to 23 percent of the sunlight striking it into electricity.

# The Flat Earth Society

The Flat Earth Society is a group that advocates the view that the Earth is a flat disc, not a sphere. It is the most widely known organization linked to modern flat-earth ideas, although the belief itself long predates the society.

## Beginnings

The society's roots go back to Samuel Birley Rowbotham, an English author who, in the mid-1800s, claimed that the Earth had no curvature, basing this on experiments he carried out along a canal known as the Bedford Level. His followers, including Lady Elizabeth Blount, later expanded on his ideas, and in 1893 she established the Universal Zetetic Society. Samuel Shenton founded the modern Flat Earth Society in England in 1956. Following Shenton's death in 1971, leadership passed to Charles K. Johnson in California, who headed it for decades until a fire wiped out its records and he died in 2001. In the 2000s the society was revived as a website and online community.

## Members' beliefs

Most forms of the model portray the Earth as a disc with the North Pole in the middle and Antarctica forming a wall of ice around the rim. The sun and moon are described as fairly small and as circling above the disc. Members usually assert that photos taken from space are fake or distorted, that governments and space agencies are part of a cover-up, and that phenomena such as ships vanishing hull-first over the horizon can be explained in other ways, for example by perspective or atmospheric effects.

## The scientific view

A vast body of evidence confirms that the Earth is spherical. This includes the fact that different constellations can be seen from different latitudes, the curved shadow Earth throws on the moon during lunar eclipses, time zones, travel around the globe by air and sea, satellite observations, and direct measurements by surveyors stretching back to Eratosthenes, who calculated Earth's circumference over two thousand years ago. No reputable scientific institution challenges this.

## Recent prominence

In the 2010s the society attracted more attention as flat-earth material circulated widely across social media and video sites. Conferences have taken place, and a number of celebrities and athletes have openly voiced sympathy for the idea. Scholars of conspiracy beliefs frequently point out that flat-earth thinking attracts people who mistrust institutions, and that the movement concerns skepticism toward authority as much as it concerns geography.

Some observers have long questioned whether the society is wholly serious. Either way, its members maintain that they are sincere, and the organization is still active today.

Cavemen did develop cavities, though far fewer than most people today, and the explanation lies mostly in what they ate. Their teeth also often wore down and fell out, but for reasons different from ours.

**Why tooth decay was uncommon**

Decay is mostly caused by bacteria that consume sugar and refined carbohydrates and give off acid that eats away enamel. Prehistoric hunter-gatherers ate hardly any of either. They lived on meat, fish, nuts, roots, wild greens and fruit in season. They sometimes had access to honey, but there was no refined sugar, no soft drinks and no sticky processed snacks clinging to teeth from morning to night.

The archaeological record backs this up. Research on skeletons dating from before farming shows few cavities. Decay grew far more widespread after agriculture took hold about 10,000 years ago, when people started consuming lots of grains like wheat, barley and maize. Starchy foods are broken down into sugars in the mouth, handing decay-causing bacteria a constant source of fuel.

**They cleaned their teeth, after a fashion**

Early humans had no toothbrushes, but they did not ignore their teeth entirely. Scientists have identified toothpick grooves on fossilized teeth of Neanderthals and earlier hominins, showing that people removed food from between their teeth. They likely chewed twigs, bones or fibrous plants too, which would have cleaned the tooth surfaces somewhat.

Beyond that, eating tough, raw or barely cooked food demanded a lot of chewing. All that vigorous chewing boosted saliva production, and saliva neutralizes acid and rinses bacteria away. Fibrous foods such as roots and raw vegetables also scour the teeth while being eaten.

**Other problems with teeth**

Prehistoric people still had dental troubles. Wear was the largest. Grit from stone grinding tools, along with dust, sand and tough food, ground the enamel away, at times exposing the pulp and causing infection. Ancient remains provide plenty of evidence of abscesses. Gum disease existed as well, and tartar, the hardened plaque that accumulates on teeth, is common on ancient teeth. Researchers now even use this calcified plaque to work out what ancient people ate and to examine their microbiomes.

Injuries made things worse, because teeth were at times used as tools for holding hides, softening materials or gripping objects. With no dentists, an infected tooth could be dangerous or even fatal.

**Did they lose their teeth?**

Some people did lose teeth, particularly older ones. But since many died young, widespread tooth loss in old age was less noticeable than it is now. People who lived into their forties or fifties frequently had worn teeth, and some were missing several.

**Where we stand now**

Curiously, people today get more cavities even though we brush, floss and see dentists. The reason is that we eat so much sugar that it overpowers our defenses. An ordinary caveman probably had healthier teeth than many people who avoid the dentist, simply because his diet was not continuously feeding the bacteria in his mouth.

In addition, oral bacteria shifted after the arrival of farming. Studies of ancient dental plaque show that the oral microbiome lost diversity as diets moved to farming and later to processed foods, and this change is associated with more disease-causing bacteria.

**In summary**

Cavemen escaped most decay because their diets were low in sugar, they chewed hard, fibrous foods, and they produced plenty of saliva. They used picks and twigs to clean their teeth, but their true advantage was what they ate. They still endured wear, abscesses and gum disease, and with no medical treatment these could become serious. For us, brushing matters because our diet differs so greatly from theirs.

Tunka-Rex is triggered by Tunka-133, which uses the Cherenkov light from the same events to determine the energy and the depth of shower maximum independently. This allows the two methods to be cross-calibrated and makes it possible to establish how accurately radio measurements can reconstruct these essential shower parameters. Special emphasis is placed on energies above 10¹⁷ eV, the range in which the transition from galactic to extragalactic cosmic rays is expected to occur. In this contribution we describe the array's layout and hardware, covering the antennas, the analog signal chain, and the data acquisition system, which uses the same infrastructure as Tunka-133. We also explain the calibration procedures and summarize the status of the experiment's first stage and its initial measurements.
