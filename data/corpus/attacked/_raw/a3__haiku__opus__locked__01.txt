The volume of a cylinder is πr²h—where V is the volume, π is about 3.14, r is the radius, and h is the height.

Why does that work? A cylinder is basically a stack of identical circles, right? Each circle has area πr². Multiply that base area by how tall it is and you get how much space is actually inside. This works for any prism—volume just equals base area times height.

Say you have a cylinder with radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Square the radius: 3² = 9. Multiply by π: 9 × 3.14 is about 28. That's your base area. Then times 10 gives you roughly 283 cubic cm.

Few things to remember: if someone gives you the diameter instead of the radius, just cut it in half first. Also watch your units—make sure the radius and height are both in the same units before you calculate. The answer comes out in cubic units like cm³ or m³.

And yeah, this formula works even if the cylinder's tilted, as long as you measure the height straight up, not along the side.

Flutter's fast mainly because of how it's built. It avoids a bunch of problems you see in other frameworks.

First, in release mode Dart gets compiled straight into native ARM or x86 code ahead of time. So there's no JavaScript interpreter running in the background slowing everything down. You get fast startup and consistent speed. For development, it does just-in-time compiling so you get hot reload.

Flutter also doesn't use the platform's built-in UI components. It draws every single pixel itself with its own rendering engine. That means no overhead from translating stuff into native widgets, and you get a solid 60 or 120 frames a second on pretty much any device.

Widgets are lightweight and immutable—they're just descriptions of what the UI looks like. Flutter compares the old widget tree to the new one and only updates whatever actually changed. Developers can optimize further with const constructors, breaking up large widgets, or using RepaintBoundary to isolate expensive repaints.

Heavy tasks can run in isolates, which are basically separate memory threads, so parsing or computation doesn't freeze up the main UI thread.

DevTools is pretty useful too—it shows you frame timing, CPU and memory usage, and tracks which widgets are causing repaints so you can find the lag.

React Native used to rely on a JavaScript bridge to native components which could cause latency. They're closing that gap with the newer architecture now. Native code in Swift or Kotlin is still the most direct access and smaller apps. Flutter's trade-off is bigger binaries and UI that doesn't automatically match how your platform looks.

Overall you get near-native performance for most apps.

Solar panels turn sunlight into electricity through something called the photovoltaic effect, which happens in a semiconductor—usually silicon.

Here's the basic setup: each solar cell is made of two layers of silicon that have been doped with other elements. The top layer's doped with phosphorus, which adds extra electrons, making it n-type or negative. The bottom one's doped with boron, which creates holes where electrons should be, making it p-type or positive. Where they meet, a p-n junction forms. Electrons near that boundary diffuse across to fill the holes, leaving behind an electric field.

Sunlight's made of photons. When a photon hits with enough energy—more than silicon's band gap of about 1.1 electron-volts—it knocks an electron loose. That creates an electron-hole pair.

The electric field at the junction acts like a one-way slope. It pushes the freed electrons one direction and the holes the other. Without that field, the electrons would just recombine with holes and release energy as heat.

Thin metal contacts on the front and back of the cell collect the separated charges. Connect them to a circuit and electrons flow through it, which makes direct current. Stack cells together into panels to bump up the voltage and power.

Direct current doesn't work for homes, so an inverter converts it to alternating current for the grid.

Most commercial panels convert somewhere around 18 to 23 percent of sunlight into electricity. The rest gets lost as heat or reflection, or the photons don't have enough or too much energy.

Arctic Silver and Thermal Grizzly go about thermal paste pretty differently. Arctic Silver's an American company that's famous for Arctic Silver 5, which has been around forever—silver-filled paste. Thermal Grizzly's from Germany and has a whole range of stuff from regular pastes to liquid metal. Also note: Arctic Silver is different from ARCTIC which makes the MX series—people mix those up constantly.

For thermal conductivity: Arctic Silver 5 doesn't officially state their numbers in W/m·K, but third-party testing usually gets around 8–9. Thermal Grizzly's more transparent—their Aeronaut is 8.5, Hydronaut is 11.8, Kryonaut is 12.5, Kryonaut Extreme is 14.2. And then Conductonaut is liquid metal at 73 W/m·K but it's electrically conductive, which matters.

The thing is, manufacturers don't measure conductivity the same way, so published numbers aren't really directly comparable. Independent testing usually shows that premium non-metallic pastes perform within a few degrees Celsius of each other. Liquid metal is the huge exception and can give you way lower temps when it's appropriate.

Arctic Silver 5 takes about 200 hours to break in under normal thermal cycling, and temperatures can drop a few degrees during that time. Thermal Grizzly pastes don't have a break-in period—they work right out of the tube, which is nice for benchmarking or short overclocking sessions. Conductonaut has no curing either, but liquid metal can get absorbed or react with some metals over time so you should check on it occasionally.

For application, Arctic Silver 5's pretty thick and they recommend a line or dot depending on your chip, then even pressure. Kryonaut and Hydronaut spread easily. Kryonaut Extreme is thicker and better for large surfaces and extreme overclocking. Conductonaut needs to be really thin and applied with a special applicator.

For high-end overclocking, Kryonaut or Kryonaut Extreme are usually better because they don't need curing and perform well under high heat. Though Kryonaut degrades faster at sustained high temps on GPUs. For daily use, you probably want something pump-out-resistant or a reapplication schedule.

Low-profile coolers don't have much pressure, so something that spreads easily and isn't conductive like Hydronaut or Kryonaut works well. Arctic Silver 5 is also reasonable if you can live with the break-in.

For delidded CPUs, Conductonaut between the die and the heat spreader gives the biggest improvement. Between the heat spreader and cooler, regular paste like Kryonaut is usually recommended.

For general builds where cost and longevity matter, Arctic Silver 5 is solid. For peak performance and enthusiast stuff, Thermal Grizzly gives more flexibility. Liquid metal should be for experienced users with delidded setups.
