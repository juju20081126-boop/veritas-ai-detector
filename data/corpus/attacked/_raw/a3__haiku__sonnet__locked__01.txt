Reason: gears aren't perfectly rigid. When you push the first gear, that push travels as a compression wave—tooth by tooth. Each gear has to bend slightly and push the next one. Cancer won't spread between people because your immune system kills foreign cells. When cancer grows in you, the tumor cells have your genetic signature so your immune system often doesn't recognize them as threats. They can spread through blood and lymph to other parts of your body. But cancer cells from someone else? Different proteins on the surface. Your immune system destroys them immediately. Should Companies Track Employee Online Activity?

Employers want to know what happens on their networks. Employees want privacy. I think companies should be able to monitor online activity at work, but with real limits.

Start with security. Company networks hold customer records, financial data, trade secrets. One employee clicks a bad link or uploads to a personal account and it's all exposed. Monitoring catches that early. Companies are legally responsible for what happens on their systems—harassment, data leaks—so they need to detect misconduct. And honestly, a company pays for working hours so it's fair to check those hours are being used for work.

But unlimited surveillance is harmful. Constant monitoring stresses people out and makes them distrustful. Research shows stressed employees are often less productive, not more. It also digs into private stuff—messages, medical searches, union organizing—things workers should keep to themselves. And data collected for security can get repurposed.

The answer is regulated monitoring. Tell employees what's tracked and why, in writing, before you start. Monitor work accounts and devices, not personal ones. Collect only what you need. Store it securely. Focus on risk patterns instead of reading every message.

With safeguards, monitoring protects both the business and workers. Without them, it turns a workplace into a place where nobody trusts anyone.
