Underwater archaeology, sometimes called maritime or nautical archaeology when it concentrates on ships and seafaring, takes the methods of land-based fieldwork into a setting that is punishing for both people and equipment. Poor visibility, powerful currents, cold, pressure and very limited time on the bottom all restrict what researchers can achieve. At the same time, these conditions can preserve organic materials, including hull timbers, rope, textiles and food remains, that rarely last on land. Since becoming a scientific discipline in the 1960s, the field has built up a refined toolkit for finding, recording, recovering and conserving submerged cultural heritage.

## Locating Sites

Research usually starts in the archives. Shipping records, insurance claims, old charts, and accounts from fishermen or sponge divers often shrink the search area; sponge divers were the first to find both the Antikythera wreck and the Late Bronze Age wreck at Uluburun, off the southern coast of Turkey. After that, systematic survey depends mainly on remote sensing:

- **Side-scan sonar** creates detailed acoustic images of the seafloor that show hull outlines, ballast mounds and scattered cargo.
- **Multibeam echosounders** collect high-resolution bathymetric data, from which three-dimensional terrain models can be built.
- **Sub-bottom profilers** send out lower-frequency acoustic pulses that pass through sediment and reveal structures buried under the seabed.
- **Marine magnetometers** pick up the magnetic anomalies caused by iron objects such as anchors, cannon and fastenings.
- **Airborne LiDAR bathymetry and satellite imagery** can chart shallow, clear coastal waters over very large areas.

Targets flagged by these surveys are then checked by eye, using either divers or remotely operated vehicles.

## Accessing the Site

In fairly shallow water, typically under about 50 meters, archaeologists can work on the seabed themselves with SCUBA or surface-supplied air. Mixed-gas diving and closed-circuit rebreathers allow greater depth and longer work periods, though they bring higher cost, more complexity and greater physiological risk. Below those limits, robotic systems become essential. Remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) are connected by a tether to a surface ship and carry high-definition cameras, lights and manipulator arms able to recover objects delicately. Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) follow pre-programmed survey patterns and gather sonar and photographic data over wide areas without constant human control. The Black Sea Maritime Archaeology Project, for instance, used this technology to document dozens of wrecks lying in deep, oxygen-poor water, including a roughly 2,400-year-old Greek merchant ship that is almost perfectly preserved.

## Recording and Documentation

Since excavation inevitably destroys context, careful recording is the foundation of all scientific work underwater. Conventional methods include baseline-offset measurement, trilateration from fixed control points, and rigid grid frames, with divers sketching on waterproof drafting film. Digital methods have increasingly added to these techniques and often replaced them. Photogrammetry, also known as structure-from-motion, merges thousands of overlapping photographs into accurate 3D models that can be measured; acoustic positioning systems give individual finds geographic coordinates; and underwater laser scanners record fine surface detail. The data that result can be analyzed long after the fieldwork ends and let the public explore sites virtually.

## Excavation and Recovery

When excavation is justified, sediment is cleared with airlifts or water dredges, which work like underwater vacuum cleaners, and the removed material is run through sieves to catch small finds. Delicate deposits are exposed by fanning the water over them by hand. Lifting bags raise heavy objects, and in shallow water a cofferdam can be built around a site so it can be dug dry, which is what happened with La Salle's ship *La Belle* in Texas in the 1990s. Occasionally whole hulls have been lifted, most famously the Swedish warship *Vasa* in 1961 and Henry VIII's warship *Mary Rose* in 1982.

## Scientific Analysis

Laboratories apply many techniques to the recovered material. Dendrochronology can date ship timbers and show where the trees were cut, and radiocarbon dating is used on other organic remains. Lead-isotope and elemental analyses help identify where metals and ceramics came from, while analysis of organic residues and ancient DNA can reveal what amphorae once held. X-ray radiography and computed tomography expose objects concealed inside corrosion concretions, and tomography in particular transformed scholars' understanding of the Antikythera Mechanism.

## Conservation

Artifacts soaked in seawater decay quickly once they meet the air, so conservation starts the moment they are raised. Objects are kept wet and desalinated to draw out soluble salts. Waterlogged wood is usually strengthened with polyethylene glycol (PEG) and then freeze-dried or dried slowly in air; *Vasa* was sprayed with PEG for seventeen years, and conserving the *Mary Rose* hull took more than thirty. Iron and copper alloys are often stabilized by electrolytic reduction.

## Preservation In Situ

The 2001 UNESCO Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage makes preservation in situ the first option to consider before any intervention. As a result, many projects now stress non-intrusive survey, protective reburial under sandbags or geotextiles, long-term monitoring of sediment movement and corrosion, and managed public access by way of underwater heritage trails. These steps address ongoing threats such as looting, commercial trawling and the climate-driven spread of wood-boring organisms.

Together, these methods reflect a wider shift in the discipline, away from treating recovery as a goal in itself and toward careful documentation, scientific interpretation and the long-term stewardship of submerged heritage.

The rotation of the Earth has no direct bearing on how long a flight takes. Because the atmosphere turns with the planet, an aircraft flies through air that is already moving at about the same speed as the ground beneath it. So an eastbound plane gets no boost from the Earth "turning beneath it," and a westbound plane suffers no penalty.

Rotation does matter indirectly, though. The Coriolis effect produced by the Earth's spin, combined with the temperature difference between the equator and the poles, helps create the jet streams, which are fast west-to-east rivers of air high in the atmosphere. In the mid-latitudes, eastbound flights can ride these tailwinds, while westbound flights have to push against them. That is why a New York to London flight usually takes about an hour less than the trip back.

Arctic Silver and Thermal Grizzly go about thermal interface materials in different ways. Arctic Silver is an American company best known for Arctic Silver 5, a silver-filled compound that has been around for a long time. Liquid metal is the obvious exception, and where it is suitable it can deliver much lower temperatures.

## Curing Time

- **Arctic Silver 5:** The manufacturer specifies a break-in period of about 200 hours of normal thermal cycling, during which temperatures can drop by a few degrees. Early readings may therefore understate how well it will eventually perform.
- **Thermal Grizzly pastes (Kryonaut, Hydronaut, Aeronaut):** These have no meaningful curing period. Right after installation they run at or close to peak performance, which suits benchmarking and short overclocking sessions.
- **Conductonaut:** It has no curing period either. Liquid metal can, however, be absorbed by or react with some metals over time, so it is wise to check it now and then.

## Application Method

- **Arctic Silver 5** is fairly thick. Depending on the layout of the processor die, the manufacturer advises a line or a small dot, followed by even mounting pressure. Since it is slightly capacitive, you should still keep it away from exposed circuitry.
- **Kryonaut and Hydronaut** spread easily and work with the dot, line or spread method. Kryonaut's lower viscosity also makes it handy for spreading by hand.
- **Kryonaut Extreme** is thicker, which makes it a better match for large, flat surfaces and for the demanding contact conditions of extreme overclocking.
- **Conductonaut** has to go on in a very thin layer, using the included applicator or swab. It conducts electricity and reacts with aluminum, so nearby surface-mount components should be protected (with conformal coating or Kapton tape, for example), and it should never be used with aluminum heatsink bases.

## Choosing for Specific Use Cases

**High-end overclocking:** For ambient and sub-ambient overclocking, Kryonaut or Kryonaut Extreme is usually the favorite, since neither needs curing and both cope well with high heat flux. Be aware that Kryonaut reportedly breaks down faster under sustained high temperatures, particularly on GPUs. On a system used every day, choose a paste that resists pump-out or plan to reapply it periodically.

**Low-profile coolers:** Compact coolers generally apply less mounting pressure and have less thermal headroom. A non-conductive paste that spreads easily, such as Hydronaut or Kryonaut, gives a thin bond line even under limited pressure. Arctic Silver 5 is also a sensible, budget-friendly pick as long as the user can live with its break-in period.

**Delidded CPUs:** Conductonaut is the established choice between the die and the integrated heat spreader, where its high conductivity makes the biggest difference. Between the heat spreader and the cooler, a conventional paste like Kryonaut is normally recommended to reduce risk. Users should make sure the IHS and cooler surfaces are copper or nickel-plated copper, and should expect some staining.

## Summary

For ordinary builds where price and durability count, Arctic Silver 5 is still a reliable choice. For peak performance from the start and for enthusiast uses, Thermal Grizzly's range is more flexible. Liquid metal is best left to experienced users working with delidded or direct-die setups.

A quick note before the list: Mariya Takeuchi made her debut in 1978, so she does belong to the late-70s scene, but most of her best-known songs date from the 1980s onward. Her career has run for more than forty years, and her signature sound is at the heart of what is now called "city pop." The songs below span her entire career, and the 1970s material is labeled so you can concentrate on it if your assignment calls for that.

## Late 1970s – Early 1980s (RCA years)

**"Modotte Oide, Watashi no Jikan" (1978)**
This was her debut single, a light, polished pop song that introduced her as a college-aged singer with a clear, warm voice. It isn't her biggest hit, but it matters as the beginning of her career.

**"September" (1979)**
One of her signature early songs, written by Takao Kisugi with lyrics by Takashi Matsumoto. It's a bittersweet song about a relationship fading as summer gives way to autumn. It brought her wide recognition and is still a fan favorite.

**"Watashi no Heart wa Stop Motion" (1979)**
This upbeat single was used in a cosmetics commercial, which helped make her a household name. It shows the bright, idol-like side of her early image.

**"Fushigi na Peach Pie" (1980)**
This is probably her biggest hit from the RCA era. It was also linked to a cosmetics campaign, and it's playful and catchy, with a sunny pop arrangement. If you need a single song to stand for her earliest fame, it's a strong pick.

**"Sweetest Music" (1980)**
Taken from the album *Miss M*, which was partly recorded in Los Angeles with American session musicians. The song shows how closely her sound was tied to West Coast AOR at that time.

## Mid-1980s (after her marriage to Tatsuro Yamashita)

She married the musician and producer Tatsuro Yamashita in 1982, stepped away from performing, and came back as a songwriter who wrote most of her own material. Yamashita produced and arranged much of her later work.

**"Plastic Love" (1984)**
From the album *Variety*. It is now her most famous song around the world. It did only modestly well when it came out, but around 2017 an unofficial YouTube upload went viral, and the song became the face of the global city pop revival. Its funky bassline, disco-tinged groove and lonely lyrics about a woman who hides her heartbreak in nightlife have been covered and remixed endlessly.

**"Mou Ichido" (1984)**
Another single from *Variety*, this mid-tempo ballad displayed her growing strength as a songwriter.

**"Genki wo Dashite" (1984 / 1987)**
She first wrote it for the actress and singer Hiroko Yakushimaru, then recorded her own version for the album *Request* (1987). It's a gentle, encouraging song that tells a friend to cheer up after a breakup, and in Japan it is one of her most beloved tracks.

## Late 1980s – 1990s

**"Single Again" (1989)**
A dramatic ballad about meeting a former lover after both people's lives have changed. It served as a TV drama theme and became one of her biggest commercial hits.

**"Kokuhaku" (1990)**
Another ballad tied to a drama, emotionally heavy and very popular, which carried on her success with mature love songs.

**"Junai Rhapsody" (1994)**
A major hit that appeared on the compilation *Impressions*, which sold several million copies and ranks among the best-selling albums in Japanese history.

**"Camouflage" (1998)**
Used as a TV drama theme, this song reached the top of the Oricon charts and proved she was still commercially strong two decades after her debut.

## 2000s and Later

**"Inochi no Uta" (2008 / 2012)**
Originally written under a pen name for an NHK drama, this song about gratitude for life later became widely known through her own recording and is often sung at ceremonies in Japan.

**Recent albums**
*Denim* (2007), *Trad* (2014) and *Precious Days* (2024) show that she has kept putting out new material, even though she rarely tours.

- If your focus is her overall popularity, "Plastic Love," "Single Again" and "Genki wo Dashite" are essential.
- If your focus is cultural impact, "Plastic Love" is the key case study, because its rediscovery online is a good example of how the internet revived interest in older Japanese pop among international audiences.

**Serena Williams' Longtime Hitting Partner Joins Azarenka's Camp**

Serena Williams and Sascha Bajin have split. For eight years Bajin was the hitting partner who helped get the world No. 1 ready for some of the biggest matches of her career.

He has now turned up in the camp of Victoria Azarenka, the former world No. 1 from Belarus and one of Williams' most persistent rivals on the WTA Tour.

The break seems to have come before the season began. Bajin was missing from Melbourne this year when Williams won the Australian Open, her 19th Grand Slam singles title. The victory put her ahead of Chris Evert and Martina Navratilova on the all-time list and three short of Steffi Graf's Open Era record of 22.

Neither Williams nor Bajin has said publicly why he moved. Hitting partners seldom get noticed outside the locker room, yet they do a great deal behind the scenes: they imitate opponents' patterns in practice, put up with hours of drills, and often act as a sounding board during long stretches on the road.

For Azarenka, signing him is a coup. Her coach, Wim Fissette, said he was delighted to bring Bajin onto the team, and he praised his experience at the sport's highest level and his knowledge of what it takes to win major titles.

Azarenka, a two-time Australian Open champion, is climbing back toward the top of the rankings after an injury-hit 2014. Fissette, who earlier coached Kim Clijsters and Simona Halep, joined her late last year.

Bajin's knowledge of Williams' game could come in handy if the two meet again. Azarenka has lost most of their matches, including two US Open finals.

That took three weeks. Her hip complained the whole time, and she told it, out loud, that it could take its complaints to Dr. Albright.
