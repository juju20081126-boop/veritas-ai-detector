When you're stuck out in the African bush, whether you live depends much more on sound choices than on heroics. Work through these priorities, more or less in this order.

### 1. Stop and think

More people die from panic than from animals. Sit, sip some water if you've got any, and assess your situation. What supplies do you have? Does anyone know your location? How soon will people notice you're missing? Your most valuable survival tool is a calm mind.

### 2. Stay where you are if possible

If you have a vehicle or aircraft, **stay beside it.** It offers shade and shelter, and it's a big target for anyone searching. Vehicles hold handy materials: mirrors for signaling, seat covers for shade, and oil and tires that burn into thick black smoke. The radiator may also contain water, but drink it only as a last resort and only if there's no antifreeze in it. Most of the people who die in these situations walked away from their vehicles. Go only when you're certain nobody is coming and you know where to find help.

### 3. Handle heat and water

In hot, dry bush, dehydration poses the greatest danger. Without water you may die within two or three days, or sooner in extreme heat.

- **Ration your sweat, not your water.** Drink as much as you need, but reduce how much fluid you lose. Rest in the shade through the hottest hours, roughly 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. Keep your clothing on to shield you from the sun, cover your head, and don't talk with your mouth open.
- **Travel only at dawn, late in the afternoon or by moonlight,** and only if you have to travel.
- **Finding water:**
  - Seek out green plants, groups of trees such as tall figs, or reeds. They frequently indicate water underground.
  - Dig in dry riverbeds, particularly on the **outer bend** of a curve, where water pools beneath the sand.
  - Observe the animals. Game trails that **merge** often lead to water. Doves and sandgrouse fly low and direct toward water at dawn and dusk. Clouds of bees or flies often mean water is nearby.
  - Gather morning dew on a piece of cloth, or rig a transpiration bag, which is a clear plastic bag tied over a leafy branch in the sun.
- **Purify water if you can** by boiling it. If the choice is between dirty water and dehydration, drink the water. Once you're rescued, diarrhea can be treated. Dehydration can't.
- **Never drink urine, alcohol or seawater,** and eat little if water is short, because digestion consumes water.

### 4. Shelter and fire

- **Shelter:** You need shade during the day and protection from cold at night, as desert and savanna nights can be surprisingly chilly. A tarp, branches or a rock overhang will do. Clear the ground of stones and look for scorpions and snakes.
- **Where not to camp:** Not on game trails, not in dry riverbeds (flash floods can come from storms many miles off), and not right beside a water source, where predators and other animals drink, particularly at night.
- **Fire:** A fire warms you, purifies water, signals to rescuers, and keeps most animals away. Gather lots of dry wood **before** dark. Thorny acacia branches can also be stacked into a protective barrier, much like the traditional *boma* herders use.

### 5. Signal for rescue

- Make **three** of anything: three fires, three rock piles, three whistle blasts. Three is the international distress signal.
- In daylight, toss green leaves or rubber onto a fire to produce heavy smoke.
- Flash a mirror, a phone screen or anything reflective at planes or faraway vehicles.
- Lay out big ground signals that stand out against the terrain, such as a large "SOS" or "X" built from rocks or branches.
- Check your phone from high ground every so often. One bar may be enough for an emergency call or text.

### 6. Respect wildlife

Most animals steer clear of people, but a handful of rules count:

- **Lions and leopards:** **Never run.** Running sets off a chase. Stand tall, make yourself look large, face the animal, shout, and retreat slowly.
- **Elephants:** Keep well away. Flared ears and a raised head can be a bluff, but a silent charge with ears flat back is genuine. Put a big tree or other obstacle between you and the animal.
- **Hippos and crocodiles:** Among the deadliest animals in Africa. Keep far from rivers and lake shores, especially at dawn, dusk and night.
- **Buffalo:** Unpredictable and aggressive, lone males most of all.
- **Snakes and scorpions:** Be careful where you put your hands and feet. Shake out shoes and clothes before wearing them. Step up onto logs and rocks rather than over them.
- **Insects:** Cover your skin at dusk to cut down on mosquito bites and the risk of malaria.

### 7. If walking out is your only choice

Move during the cooler hours and head in a clear line toward help. Roads, tracks, fences, power lines and rivers (kept at a safe distance) typically lead to people. Watch for signs of herders or villages, like livestock, smoke and footpaths. Local people are frequently your best hope of rescue, so approach them calmly and ask for help.

### 8. Food is last

You can last for weeks without eating, so don't gamble on unfamiliar plants, mushrooms or berries, since many are poisonous. Insects such as termites, and certain known safe foods such as baobab fruit, can help, but water, shelter and rescue come before anything else.

### Above all

Preparation is the best survival tool. Before heading into the bush, tell someone your route and when you'll be back, bring extra water and a basic kit, and travel with an experienced local guide when you can.

The bootstrap probability, meaning how often bootstrap replicates support a hypothesis, is commonly used to assess this, yet it is known to be biased. Multiscale bootstrap resamples at several sample sizes and extrapolates to a hypothetical negative sample size, which yields approximately unbiased p-values that fix this bias. In practice, though, hypotheses are often picked for testing precisely because the observed data support them, and overlooking this selection inflates false positive rates.
```dockerfile
# 1. Begin with an official, lightweight Node.js base image
FROM node:20-alpine

# 2. Set the container's working directory
WORKDIR /usr/src/app

# 3. Copy just the dependency manifests first
COPY package*.json ./

# 4. Install the production dependencies
RUN npm ci --omit=dev

# 5. Copy over the remaining application source code
COPY . .

# 6. Tell Node (and many libraries) to run in production mode
ENV NODE_ENV=production

# 7. Document the port the app listens on
EXPOSE 3000

# 8. Run as the unprivileged "node" user instead of root
USER node

# 9. Define the command that launches the app
CMD ["node", "server.js"]
```

## What each part does

**`FROM node:20-alpine`**
Each Docker image is built on top of another one. This line picks the official Node.js 20 image running on Alpine Linux, which is tiny (tens of megabytes rather than hundreds). Node and npm are already installed.

**`WORKDIR /usr/src/app`**
Makes the directory if it isn't there and sets it as the current directory for every instruction after it. This keeps your app's files tidy and stops anything from being written to the container's root.

**`COPY package*.json ./`**
Puts `package.json` and `package-lock.json` into the image *ahead of* the rest of your code. The reason is **layer caching**: Docker caches every step, so if these files haven't changed, it can skip reinstalling dependencies on the next build. Rebuilds are much quicker when only source files have been edited.

**`RUN npm ci --omit=dev`**
Installs dependencies exactly as pinned in `package-lock.json`. `npm ci` is quicker and more reproducible than `npm install`, and `--omit=dev` leaves out devDependencies such as test frameworks and linters, which aren't needed at runtime.

**`COPY . .`**
Brings the rest of your project into the working directory.

**`ENV NODE_ENV=production`**
Defines an environment variable. Frameworks like Express read it to switch on optimizations such as view caching and terser error messages.

**`EXPOSE 3000`**
Records that the app listens on port 3000. It doesn't really publish the port; you still need `-p` when you run the container.

**`USER node`**
The official Node images ship with a non-root user named `node`. Running as that user limits the harm if your app is ever compromised.

**`CMD ["node", "server.js"]`**
The default command executed when the container starts. Swap `server.js` for your own entry file. Invoking `node` directly (instead of `npm start`) means your app properly receives shutdown signals such as `SIGTERM`.

## Add a `.dockerignore`

Put a `.dockerignore` file beside the Dockerfile so that `COPY . .` doesn't drag in files you don't want:

```
node_modules
npm-debug.log
.git
.env
Dockerfile
.dockerignore
```

Leaving out `node_modules` matters most: your local modules might be built for another OS, and the image installs its own regardless.

## Build and run it

```bash
docker build -t my-node-app .
docker run -p 3000:3000 my-node-app
```

The first command builds an image tagged `my-node-app`. The second launches a container and maps port 3000 on your machine to port 3000 inside it, so you can open `http://localhost:3000`.

If your app has a build step (TypeScript, a bundler, and so on), you would normally move to a multi-stage build: install every dependency and compile in a first stage, then copy only the compiled output and production dependencies into a slim final image.

The array's first stage is made up of antenna stations spread over the densely instrumented core of Tunka-133, each with two perpendicularly oriented SALLA (Short Aperiodic Loaded Loop Antenna) elements, selected because their gain depends only weakly on ground conditions. The antennas are read out by the existing Tunka-133 data-acquisition system and triggered by the Cherenkov detectors, which keeps the extra hardware simple and cheap. This contribution covers the array layout, the design of the antennas and electronics, the absolute calibration procedure, and the performance expected from air-shower simulations. The results will show whether radio detection, which unlike optical methods is not limited to clear, moonless nights, can offer a cost-effective way of measuring the energy and composition of cosmic rays above about 10¹⁷ eV.

**The Wrong Century**

The last thing Mara Okafor could remember was the lab's countdown hitting zero and somebody shouting about a voltage spike. The next thing she registered was a smell of wet straw, woodsmoke and something far worse, which she later found out was a pigsty.

She sat up in a muddy field. Somewhere a church bell was ringing. Two boys in rough wool tunics were watching her from behind a stone wall, and one of them crossed himself.

Her wrist display was dead. Her phone had no signal. She checked it again, which was pointless, and then laughed out loud, since the alternative was screaming.

Judging by the shape of the church tower and the language the boys yelled as they ran off, she guessed England, sometime around the 1300s. She had studied the period for exactly one semester in college and mostly remembered that people died of things that were easy to treat in her own era.

The first lesson came in the first week: knowledge is worthless if you can't explain where it came from. When she tried telling the village reeve to boil the drinking water, he stared as though she had proposed baptizing the well. So she quit explaining and just did it, in front of everyone. She brewed weak ale the way the locals did, since brewing meant boiling too. She scrubbed her hands until the baker's wife began calling her "the clean woman." It wasn't a compliment.

The second lesson took longer: everything moves slowly. To write a letter, she had to find someone who could read it. Starting a fire cost her twenty minutes and three burned fingers, until old Agnes, the widow who had reluctantly taken her in, showed her how to use flint and dry fungus. Mara had once designed parts of a quantum relay. Now a woman who couldn't sign her own name was teaching her, and Agnes was the better teacher.

There was no streaming, no scrolling, nothing to fill the quiet. The evenings were long and dark, so people talked. They told stories, sang, and argued about the weather and the lord's new taxes. Mara found that she remembered more of her grandmother's old songs than she ever had in her own century, and she started singing them by the fire. The children adored them.

She made mistakes. She almost got herself accused of witchcraft by setting a broken arm with too much confidence. Only the miller's testimony that he had seen her pray at Mass saved her. After that, she learned to present every useful idea as something "my mother's people did," and to let others take the credit when they wanted it.

By midsummer her hands were calloused. She could tell rain was coming from the ache in the air and the way the swallows flew. She knew which herbs Agnes used for fever and which were only for show.

One autumn evening, a strange shimmer appeared at the edge of the field where she had first woken, and her dead wrist display flickered to life. *Retrieval window: 90 seconds.*

Mara stood in the doorway of Agnes's cottage for a long time. Then she went inside, kissed the old woman's forehead, and walked toward the light, carrying a pouch of dried yarrow that she had no plan to explain to anyone back home.
