Autorotation describes the maneuver a helicopter operator performs to bring the craft down securely when mechanical power is lost or the rear rotor breaks. Rather than relying on the engine, the primary rotor stays in motion because the aircraft is falling and air streams upward across it. The operator then draws power stored in the spinning rotor to reduce landing impact.

It resembles how a sycamore fruit spins during its fall. During regular flying, the rotor disk pulls air downward. In autorotation, descent drives air upward across the disk, spinning the blades. Rotor velocity must stay in safe parameters: too sluggish and lift disappears, too fast and metal can break.

**Standard autorotation procedure**

1. **Spot the malfunction.** Signs include an alert buzzer or indicator light, an engine tone change, or the nose twisting as torque vanishes. Fast recognition is critical.

2. **Drop the lever immediately.** This cuts the main rotor pitch, lowering wind resistance and sustaining rotor motion. Response time is vital—rotor velocity drops instantly.

3. **Monitor rotor pace.** Checking the speed gauge, the operator adjusts the lever to hold pace in the acceptable band.

4. **Settle into descent angle.** With controls, the operator establishes ideal descent velocity, which differs by helicopter and is roughly between 60 to 80 mph. Velocity and rotor pace are primary.

5. **Identify touch-down location.** The operator picks a spot, best into the breeze and clear of hazards, and steers toward it, balancing with pedals.

6. **Alert others and get ready.** When able, the operator sends a distress signal and runs through potential reasons—like attempting an engine restart.

7. **Raise the nose.** At about 40 to 100 feet up, the operator tugs the controls back to angle the nose upward. This decreases both movement and falling rate and raises rotor pace.

8. **Straighten up.** Just before hitting ground, the operator uses controls to balance the helicopter for landing on skids or wheels.

9. **Slow the descent.** Near touchdown, the operator pulls the lever to use remaining rotor momentum, slowing the drop and softening arrival.

10. **Settle and stop.** Upon landing, the operator holds controls steady, fully lowers the lever, and brings the helicopter to rest.

Operators practice autorotations extensively, typically finishing with an engine restart, making the move automatic.
