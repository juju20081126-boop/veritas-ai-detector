Working from Home: A Mixed Blessing

In recent years, remote work has gone from an occasional privilege to a routine way of life for millions of people. The shift offers genuine advantages, but it also raises problems that employees and companies alike have to take seriously.

Cutting out the commute is the most visible gain. Someone who used to sit in traffic for an hour in each direction recovers about ten hours every week, which can be spent with family, on exercise, or simply resting. Working remotely is also more flexible: people can build their schedule around the hours when they work best or around obligations like looking after children or aging parents. Companies, for their part, save on office expenses and can recruit from a far larger geographic pool, because they are no longer restricted to candidates who live close by.

Still, there are serious downsides to working from home. Isolation is a common complaint, as workers lose the informal chats and feeling of belonging that a shared workplace provides. Over time this can hurt mental health and erode relationships within a team. Collaboration can suffer too. Impromptu problem-solving and mentoring come more easily face to face than through scheduled video calls, and junior staff who are still learning may be hit hardest. Lastly, the line between job and home can disappear. With the kitchen table doubling as a desk, switching off gets harder, and many remote employees say they end up working more hours, not fewer.

Taking all this into account, remote work is clearly neither a flawless answer nor a passing fad. How well it works depends largely on the kind of job and the temperament of the person doing it. For many organizations, a hybrid arrangement that pairs the focus and freedom of home with the human contact of the office could be the best compromise. The task now is to design setups that keep the advantages while holding down the costs.

Whether Mercy is the right pick for a particular match has much more to do with your team composition, the game mode, and your own abilities than with where your teammates come from. Players in China, or anywhere else, do not need any specific support hero. Even so, a few practical points deserve attention when you are teaming up with people who may not speak your language.

**Where Mercy shines in these situations**

Few heroes rely less on spoken coordination than Mercy. Her basic routine of healing a wounded ally, boosting damage with the blue beam, and reviving fallen teammates is mostly reactive: you act on what you see on screen instead of on callouts. The ping system and communication wheel carry most of what she needs, like "I need healing" or "Enemy spotted," and those messages are localized automatically. That makes her a sensible choice when language differences get in the way of voice chat.

Guardian Angel also lets her stay close to aggressive teammates. When your team likes a fast, dive-heavy style, which is common on Asian servers, Mercy's ability to remain attached to a flanking Genji, Echo, or Pharah can be especially useful.

**Where she falls short**

Compared with Ana, Kiriko, or Baptiste, Mercy's healing numbers are modest, and she brings almost no crowd control or burst damage. When the opposing team is pressing hard, or your tanks need a lot of sustain, she can have a tough time. Her resurrection is strong but leaves her vulnerable, and skilled opponents will go after her for it.

**Verdict**

Mercy is a good call when your team has strong damage dealers who can make use of the boost, when your fellow support can supply more burst healing, and when communication is mostly non-verbal. If your team lacks dependable damage or your tank is struggling, a hero with more healing throughput or utility might serve you better.

In the end, the smartest move is to watch which heroes your teammates favor and how they play during the first fight, then adapt. Being flexible matters more than any one hero choice, and it is the most useful thing a support player can offer.

Practical Skills or Theory at University?

Nowadays a lot of students attend university because they hope to get a good job later. For this reason, people disagree about what universities ought to teach. Some think universities should focus on practical job skills, while others believe theoretical knowledge matters more. In this essay I will look at both sides and then give my own opinion.

First, practical skills are very useful. When students graduate, companies expect them to begin work fast. If a student already know how to use computer programs, write reports or work in a team, the company don't need to spend a long time training them. Besides, many students pay a lot of money for university, so they want to study things they can use in real life. For instance, my cousin studied business, but he said he was never taught how to make a simple budget in Excel. After he got a job, he had to learn it by himself.

Second, theoretical knowledge is important too. Theory helps students understand why things happen, not just how to do them. Technology changes very fast, so a skill that is useful today maybe will be out of date in five years. But if students understand the basic ideas, they can learn new skills more easy. For example, doctors and engineers need a strong theory background, because without it they can make dangerous mistakes.

My opinion is that universities should teach both, with theory as the foundation. Students can pick up some practical skills through internships or part-time jobs, and universities can help them find these opportunities. Deep knowledge, however, is difficult to get alone, so that is the main job of a university.

To conclude, practical skills and theory both have advantages. I think a good university gives students strong knowledge and also some chance to practice, so that they are ready for work and for the future.

The parrot was called Doctor, and he had been around longer than the marriage.

Walt had picked him up in 1979 from a man at a flea market, who promised the bird could recite the Gettysburg Address. He could not. But within a month of arriving home, he could do Walt perfectly: the throat-clearing before a phone call, the soft whistle when a pot was about to boil over, and most of all, the line Walt hollered each morning as he yanked open the kitchen curtains.

"Boy, its a joyous day outside!"

He said it when it rained. He said it when it sleeted. He said it the February the furnace broke and they ate breakfast in their coats. June would roll her eyes and point out that it was eight degrees and the sky looked like dishwater, and Walt would reply that this was exactly what made it joyous, since any fool could be happy when the sun was out.

Doctor picked it up in Walt's own voice, gravel included, and for forty-four years the pair of them, man and bird, shouted it together while June buttered toast and acted annoyed.

Walt died in March.

It happened fast, which everyone assured June was a mercy, and she figured it was. The funeral was packed. Casseroles arrived and were eaten. Her daughter stayed a week before heading back to Phoenix, and after that the house went quiet in a way June had never known, a quiet with corners.

On her first morning alone, she left the curtains shut. She sat in the dim kitchen while her coffee went cold, thinking of nothing at all.

Behind her, Doctor shuffled on his perch.

"Boy," he said, in Walt's voice, "its a joyous day outside!"

June let go of her cup, and it fell.

She had forgotten. That was the foolish part. Through all the arrangements, the phone calls, the paperwork, it had slipped her mind that the bird knew how to be him.

She kept the curtains closed that day and the next. Doctor said it regardless, every morning at seven, cheerful and sure of himself, as though nothing had happened. As though Walt were simply in the next room. Now and then June would reply before she could stop herself, "It's eight degrees, you old fool," and then hear her own words and have to sit down.

On the phone, her daughter gently suggested that the bird might be better off with someone else. A rescue, perhaps. A place with other parrots.

"He's fine," June said.

"Mom. Is he fine, or are you?"

June did not answer.

In truth, she had begun to wait for it. She woke at a quarter to seven and lay there listening. She dreaded the morning he would stay silent, and dreaded even more the morning he would speak, because every time the voice came, her heart forgot for half a second. Then it remembered. Each day she lost him all over again.

One morning in May, light came through the gap in the curtains and laid a long gold bar across the linoleum. Doctor said it, and June rose and crossed to the window.

She pulled the curtains open.

It was truly lovely out. The lilacs Walt had planted were in bloom. A robin worked the lawn. The sky was that clean, rinsed blue that follows a night of rain.

"Boy," Doctor said behind her, "its a joyous day outside."

"I know," June whispered. "I know it is."

She stayed at the window for a long while, letting the sun find her, a joyous day he would never get to see, while the bird went on saying it in his voice, again and again, as if he were trying to teach her the words.

**Gunmen Kill at Least 23 at Tunisia's Bardo Museum**

TUNIS, Tunisia — Gunmen burst into the Bardo National Museum in the Tunisian capital on Wednesday and killed at least 23 people, striking at one of the nation's most famous cultural sites and at the tourism industry that its economy relies on.

The majority of the victims were foreign tourists, a number of whom had disembarked that morning from cruise ships moored in Tunis. Security forces brought a standoff inside the museum to an end by killing two of the assailants, and dozens of people received treatment for injuries. The Bardo, home to one of the finest collections of Roman mosaics anywhere, stands beside the national parliament.

The assault has rattled a country frequently called the lone success story of the Arab Spring. Following the 2011 revolution that drove out President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali, Tunisia wrote a new constitution and held free elections. But it has also become a leading supplier of foreign fighters to extremist groups in Syria and Iraq, where an estimated 3,000 Tunisians are thought to have gone.

Mabrouka M'Barek, a former member of Tunisia's National Constituent Assembly, sees those two facts as tightly connected. She believes Tunisia has failed to "recruit" its own people into society, so that many young people, especially in the poorer interior, feel they have no stake in the new democracy. If the state gives them no place, she contends, extremist groups will gladly supply one.

Her view moves attention away from policing and toward joblessness, inequality between regions and political exclusion, the very grievances that drove the 2011 uprising.

Tunisia's leaders have promised a forceful response, and investigators are trying to determine who planned the attack. Yet as the country grieves, many Tunisians wonder whether their young democracy can get to the next generation before the recruiters do.

Reaching for the Stars Without Neglecting Home

Each year, governments pour billions into rockets, probes, and telescopes, even as millions of people go without clean water, medical care, or education. Critics naturally argue that this money should be spent on Earth. But setting space exploration and earthly problems against each other misreads how progress works in both.

The argument against space funding carries real emotional force. One Mars mission could pay for hospitals or vaccination drives. While people are suffering today, money for faraway planets can look like a luxury that only rich countries can afford.

Yet space budgets are smaller than many people think. NASA gets under half a percent of the U.S. federal budget, a sliver of what defense or health programs receive. Even more important, space research pays for itself in practical ways. Satellites follow hurricanes, watch over crops, and measure climate change. Technology originally built for spaceflight has improved water purification, medical imaging, and solar power. Space programs also motivate students to go into science and engineering.

Governments should keep funding space exploration, then, but do so wisely, giving priority to missions with clear scientific or social value and working with other nations to split the costs. In the end, looking up often helps us look after the ground beneath us.

Feeds of products on big e-commerce platforms mix organic recommendations with sponsored items, and ads have usually been positioned to maximize immediate advertising revenue. This focus on the short term ignores an important side effect: badly placed or too many ads can reduce how much users engage with organic content, which lowers the platform's value over time. In this paper, we cast ad allocation in product feeds as a sequential decision problem, aiming to maximize organic traffic subject to limits on ad exposure and revenue. We present a reinforcement learning framework where an agent decides, for every request, how many ads to place in the feed and where, based on the user's context and the list of candidate items. Because engagement signals are sparse and delayed, we build a reward that blends immediate click feedback with a learned estimate of how deeply users browse afterward, and we use a constrained policy optimization method to meet platform-wide ad targets. Offline experiments on large-scale logged data and online A/B tests on a leading e-commerce platform show that our approach considerably raises organic clicks and session length while preserving ad revenue, beating fixed-slot and greedy allocation baselines.

FeSe enters a nematic phase near 90 K without any long-range magnetic order, which makes it an ideal system for isolating the electronic origin of rotational symmetry breaking in iron-based superconductors. We use high-resolution angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy on mechanically detwinned single crystals to map how the electronic structure changes across the nematic transition, resolved by momentum and orbital. At low temperature, the d_xz/d_yz splitting at the Brillouin zone corner reaches roughly 50 meV, setting the characteristic nematic energy scale, whereas the splitting near the zone center is much smaller, which points to a strongly momentum-dependent order parameter. Remarkably, the nematic state shows just one electron pocket; the pocket expected at the Y point cannot be detected within our experimental resolution. We examine how orbital-selective correlations and the d_xy orbital contribute to this suppression, and we argue that the resulting anisotropic Fermi surface naturally explains both the strongly anisotropic superconducting gap and the lack of magnetic order in FeSe.
