Underwater archaeology uses terrestrial archaeology methods on sites below seas, lakes, rivers. Since submerged environments preserve organic materials that decay on land, shipwrecks and drowned settlements give you exceptionally rich evidence of past societies. But you need specialized techniques and technologies.

**Finding sites**

Start with searching. Combine archival research, historical charts, reports from fishers and divers with geophysical survey:

- **Side-scan sonar** generates detailed acoustic images of the seabed showing wreck mounds and debris.
- **Multibeam echosounders** create high-resolution bathymetric maps showing a wreck's form and relationship to surrounding seafloor.
- **Sub-bottom profilers** penetrate sediment to detect buried structures.
- **Marine magnetometers** detect ferrous objects like anchors and cannon.

In shallow coastal waters, airborne LiDAR can map drowned landscapes and nearshore wrecks.

**Robotic systems**

Where depth or hazards exceed diver limits, you use remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) and autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs). ROVs are tethered to ships and carry cameras, lighting, sonar, manipulator arms so archaeologists can inspect and recover objects in real time. AUVs follow pre-programmed paths and can map extensive areas with precision. This lets you study deep-water sites like the well-preserved wrecks in the Black Sea.

**Diving**

For sites within diver reach, specialized equipment extends working time and depth. SCUBA in shallow water, mixed-gas and closed-circuit rebreathers for longer and deeper dives. Saturation diving lets personnel work at depth for extended periods with one decompression at the end.

**Excavation**

Underwater excavation follows the same rigorous principles as land work. You establish a grid so every find gets recorded in three dimensions, remove sediment carefully without damaging fragile material. Acoustic positioning and offset measurements record precise artifact locations.

Documentation's been transformed by digital methods. Photogrammetry turns overlapping photographs into accurate three-dimensional models so a site gets recorded in detail and shared with scholars through virtual reconstructions. Laser scanning does similar work for individual artifacts.

**Conservation**

Objects that've lain underwater for centuries are unstable once recovered and need immediate conservation. Waterlogged wood must stay wet and get impregnated with consolidants before drying. Iron objects encased in marine concretion get X-rayed before cleaning.

Scientific analysis complements this. Dendrochronology and radiocarbon dating provide chronological frameworks. Residue analysis and DNA recovery reveal cargo contents and trade patterns.

**Legal and ethical**

Technological capability must match responsible practice. The UNESCO 2001 Convention establishes preservation in situ as the first option and prohibits commercial exploitation. Archaeologists weigh excavation research value against the risk of irreversible loss, and non-invasive survey is increasingly preferred.

**Is it ethical to use animals in research?**

Every medicine you take was probably tested on an animal. So is it right to use animals for humans? I think yes, but only with strict rules and alternatives whenever possible.

The main argument for animal research is human health benefits. The polio vaccine, insulin, heart surgery—all developed with animal studies. Animals share biological features with us so scientists can see how treatments affect a living body in ways petri dishes can't. Millions are alive because of this.

But the opposition has strong points. Animals feel pain, fear, distress like we do. Many think it's wrong to make a creature suffer, especially when it can't consent. Plus animals aren't humans, so drugs that work on mice often fail in people. So some animal suffering doesn't lead anywhere useful.

The best approach is the "three Rs": replace animals with other methods when possible, reduce the number used, refine experiments to cause less pain. Scientists can use computer models and lab-grown tissues for some tests now. Governments should require ethics committees to review every experiment.

Using animals in research can be justified when necessary to save lives but it shouldn't be done carelessly. If scientists treat animals with respect and keep searching for better options, we protect both human health and our values.

Christopher Columbus reached the Americas in **1492**, landing October 12 on an island in the Bahamas the Lucayans called Guanahani. He renamed it San Salvador. Historians still debate which island exactly—Samana Cay and present-day San Salvador are the main candidates.

Columbus was a Genoese navigator who believed he could reach Asia by sailing west. After Portugal turned him down he got support from Queen Isabella and King Ferdinand, who financed the expedition. He sailed from Spain August 3, 1492 with three ships: the Nina, Pinta, Santa Maria. After stops in the Canary Islands the fleet spent over a month crossing the ocean.

After landing in the Bahamas, Columbus explored several islands including Cuba and Hispaniola. He thought he'd reached Asia's outskirts, which is why he called them the Indies and why indigenous people got called "Indians." The Santa Maria ran aground off Hispaniola on Christmas Day, and its wood built a small settlement named La Navidad. Columbus returned to Spain in early 1493 with plants, gold ornaments and several captive Taino people.

Columbus made three more trips in 1493, 1498 and 1502. During these voyages he explored more of the Caribbean and reached coasts of Central and South America. He never accepted he'd found a new continent.

Columbus wasn't the first from the Eastern Hemisphere to reach the Americas. Norse explorers led by Leif Erikson settled briefly at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland around 1000. Indigenous peoples lived there for thousands of years before either.

But the 1492 voyage started sustained contact between Europe and the Americas. This had enormous consequences—exchange of crops and animals, disease that devastated indigenous populations, start of European colonization. Columbus's legacy is widely debated now, with historians discussing both his navigational achievements and the violence and enslavement that followed.
