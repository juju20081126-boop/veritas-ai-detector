Quantum mechanics forecasts experimental outcomes with remarkable accuracy, yet it stays silent on what is "really" going on underneath. The leading interpretations are:

- **Copenhagen:** The wavefunction is a device for predicting results. Measurement "collapses" it, and questions about what occurs between measurements are often regarded as meaningless.
- **Many-Worlds (Everett):** Collapse never happens. Every possible outcome is realized, and the universe splits into separate worlds that don't interact.
- **Pilot-Wave (de Broglie–Bohm):** Particles always hold definite positions and are steered by a real wave. The theory is deterministic but nonlocal.
- **Objective Collapse (e.g., GRW, Penrose):** Collapse is a genuine physical process. This alters the theory in ways that might be testable.
- **QBism:** The wavefunction expresses an agent's personal beliefs about outcomes rather than physical reality.
- **Relational QM (Rovelli):** Quantum states exist only in relation to other systems, so no observer-independent state exists.

They differ over realism, determinism, locality and the observer's role. These are good themes around which to build your assignment.

Within the yogic and tantric traditions of India, chakras (the Sanskrit word means "wheels") are hubs of energy and awareness in the subtle body, the nonphysical aspect of a person said to permeate the physical one. They are typically imagined as spinning wheels or lotus flowers set along a central channel, the *sushumna nadi*, that stretches from the base of the spine up to the top of the head. Traditions differ in how they explain the chakras' role, yet most share a central idea: the chakras chart consciousness, and spiritual development means drawing awareness upward through them while keeping the entire system balanced.

## Where the idea comes from

Chakras are found in Hindu and Buddhist tantric texts dating from about the 8th century, and the early sources disagree about their number; there are systems with four, five, six, or many more. The seven-chakra scheme familiar today derives chiefly from a 16th-century Sanskrit work, the *Ṣaṭ-cakra-nirūpaṇa*, which was brought to Western readers by Sir John Woodroffe's 1919 book *The Serpent Power*. Many of the popular associations, including the rainbow colors, ties to particular glands, and psychological themes for each center, were supplied by Western authors in the 20th century. This does not make the modern version worthless, but it is a mixture of an ancient framework and more recent interpretation.

## The seven chakras as a path

- **Root (Muladhara)**: base of the spine; safety, survival, grounding
- **Sacral (Svadhisthana)**: lower abdomen; emotion, pleasure, creativity
- **Solar plexus (Manipura)**: navel; willpower, confidence, agency
- **Heart (Anahata)**: middle of the chest; love, compassion, forgiveness
- **Throat (Vishuddha)**: communication, honest self-expression
- **Third eye (Ajna)**: between the brows; insight, intuition
- **Crown (Sahasrara)**: top of the head; transcendence, union with the divine

Read upward, the list traces a journey. A person starts by needing stability and safety, then a healthy bond with feeling and desire, then a self from which to act. With that groundwork, the heart can open toward others, and after it come authentic expression, deeper insight, and finally a feeling of connection that goes beyond the individual self. Numerous teachers point out how closely this parallels developmental psychology, with Maslow's hierarchy of needs a common comparison.

## Kundalini and awakening

In tantric yoga the chakras also serve as the stages of *kundalini*, a sleeping spiritual energy pictured as a serpent coiled at the base of the spine. Practice is said to make kundalini climb the central channel and pierce each chakra in order. On reaching the crown, Shakti (the dynamic, feminine principle) joins with Shiva (pure consciousness), and the practitioner achieves liberation. From this perspective the chakras are more than symbols of growth; they are the actual machinery of awakening, which is why traditional teachers treated kundalini practice carefully and demanded experienced guidance.

## How practitioners work with them

Typical practices include:

- **Meditation and visualization**, placing attention on each center in turn
- **Mantra**, particularly the seed syllables LAM, VAM, RAM, YAM, HAM, and OM
- **Breathwork (pranayama)**, thought to move vital energy along the subtle channels
- **Yoga postures** selected to open specific areas of the body
- **Self-inquiry**, taking each chakra's themes as prompts for reflection

The final practice may be the most valuable. Even with no metaphysical beliefs, the chakras hand people a language for asking where they feel stuck. Is it missing security? Trouble with intimacy? Fear of speaking up?

## Balance, not just ascent

Many contemporary teachers emphasize that spiritual growth is not a race to the crown. A person who pursues transcendent experiences while ignoring relationships, money, or unresolved grief risks what psychologist John Welwood termed "spiritual bypassing." The chakra model resists this: the lower centers are not hurdles to climb past but foundations to reinforce. Maturity, in this view, is integration, being grounded and open together.

## A note on evidence

No scientific evidence shows that chakras exist as physical structures, and claims connecting them to nerve plexuses or endocrine glands are speculative. Yet many of the practices built around them do have research behind them: meditation, breathwork, and yoga have measurable effects on stress, attention, and mood. For many people, chakras serve best as a contemplative map. They are less a description of anatomy than an organized way to attend to the body, examine the mind, and move toward a fuller, more compassionate life.

Prior to the 1800s, most people did not "apply" for work the way we do now. The great majority were employed in farming or a family trade, and jobs were generally found through personal contacts and face-to-face deals instead of written applications.

**How people learned about openings**

- **Word of mouth.** Relatives, neighbors, fellow parishioners, and other workers were the chief source of news. Taverns, coffeehouses, and market days acted as informal job boards.
- **Hiring fairs.** In England, yearly "hiring" or "mop" fairs drew farm laborers and servants into market towns. Workers carried a symbol of their trade (a shepherd might hold a tuft of wool, a carter a length of whipcord, a maid a mop) and were usually taken on for a year.
- **Apprenticeship.** Guilds controlled the skilled trades. Parents arranged for a child, often about 14 years old, to be bound by contract (an indenture) to a master for roughly seven years in return for training, food, and lodging.
- **Newspapers.** By the 1700s, papers in London and colonial American cities ran advertisements for clerks, servants, and tutors, and job seekers sometimes placed notices about themselves.
- **Register offices.** Cities developed early employment agencies. In 1749, the novelist Henry Fielding and his brother John opened London's Universal Register Office, which, among other services, matched servants with employers for a fee.

**How people applied**

Most hiring took place in person. A laborer turned up at a farm, workshop, or dock and was assessed on appearance, strength, and skill. For domestic service and other trusted positions, a "character" (a reference from a former employer) was essential. More educated candidates, such as clerks, secretaries, or tutors, wrote letters of application by hand, and tidy penmanship was itself a qualification. Many government, church, and military posts relied on patronage: the support of a powerful sponsor often counted for more than ability.

**An important caveat**

A great deal of labor in this period was not freely chosen. Indentured servitude, parish apprenticeships imposed on poor children, and slavery made up a large share of work, especially in the Americas, so any discussion of "job hunting" in this era should acknowledge that many workers had very little choice.

How long a flight takes is not directly affected by the Earth's rotation. Because the atmosphere turns with the planet, an aircraft travels through air that is already moving at nearly the same speed as the ground beneath it. An eastbound plane therefore gets no boost from the Earth "turning beneath it," and a westbound plane suffers no penalty.

Rotation still matters, though, in an indirect and important way. The Coriolis effect produced by the planet's spin, along with the temperature difference between the equator and the poles, helps create the jet streams, which are fast upper-atmosphere currents of air flowing from west to east. At mid-latitudes, eastbound flights can ride these tailwinds, while westbound flights have to push against them. Offside is among football's most significant rules, and among the most disputed. It falls under **Law 11** of the Laws of the Game. Its aim is to prevent attackers from just loitering near the opponent's goal waiting for a long ball.

### Being in an offside position

A player is in an **offside position** if, at the instant a teammate plays or touches the ball:

1. They are in the **opponent's half** of the pitch (the halfway line is treated as part of your own half), **and**
2. Any part of their head, body or feet is **nearer the opponent's goal line** than both the ball **and** the second-last opponent.

The second-last opponent is normally the last outfield defender, since the goalkeeper is usually the final opponent. Arms and hands are ignored when judging position, because players cannot legally play the ball with them in any case.

You are **onside** if you are level with the second-last opponent, level with the ball, or behind the ball.

### Position alone isn't an offence

Merely standing in an offside position brings no penalty. A player is only penalized if they get **involved in active play** by:

- **Interfering with play**: playing or touching a ball that a teammate has passed or touched.
- **Interfering with an opponent**: for instance, blocking the goalkeeper's view, challenging for the ball, or making a clear action that affects an opponent's ability to play it.
- **Gaining an advantage**: playing a ball that bounces or deflects to them off the post, the crossbar or an opponent, or one the goalkeeper has saved.

### Exceptions

You **cannot** be offside when you take the ball directly from:

- a **goal kick**
- a **throw-in**
- a **corner kick**

### The penalty

Once offside is called, the defending side is awarded an **indirect free kick** at the place where the offence occurred.

### A quick example

A striker races past the last defender **before** her teammate plays the pass. When the ball is played, she is closer to the goal line than both the defender and the ball. That is offside. Had she timed her run to be level with or behind the defender when the pass was made, she would be onside, even if she finished well beyond the defence a second later.

The main point to remember is that offside is judged **when the ball is played**, not when the player receives it. That is why so many offside decisions come down to fractions of a second, and why VAR replays freeze the frame at the exact moment of the pass.

She changed only as anyone would over ten years: some silver at the temples, a bit more care in rising from a chair. The color never changed.

Whenever I brought it up, my parents traded a look I was not yet able to read, and the topic was dropped. By sixteen I had stopped mentioning her at all, having privately decided that I was either the subject of an extraordinary coincidence or the author of a minor delusion.

On the evening of my eighteenth birthday, my father asked me to come to the study. My mother was already there, and next to her, in a chair carried in from the dining room, sat the woman in blue.

"This is Mrs. Eleanor Hartwell," my father said. "She was your grandfather's attorney. She is now, I suppose, yours."

Mrs. Hartwell bowed her head slightly. Up close, her eyes were grey and remarkably direct. "I owe you an explanation," she said, "and I have waited twelve years to give it."

My grandfather, whom I never met, had died the spring before I was born. He left a sizeable estate in trust for me, with one condition: someone he selected was to watch me from time to time throughout my childhood, without interfering, and report on my character before the money was released. He had not wanted, she explained, to hand his life's work to a stranger. Unable to know me himself, he had appointed someone to know me for him.

"Why blue?" I asked, since it was the only question I could put together.

For the first time, she smiled. "Your grandfather's instruction. He said that if the boy was observant, he would notice. And that an observant boy was the only kind worth trusting with anything."

She opened a leather folder and set it on the desk between us. Inside, in a neat and unhurried hand, were twelve years of notes: the pail at Cape May, a waiter I had thanked in Lisbon, a younger child I had helped onto the ferry.

"He would have approved of you," she said. "I certainly do."

## History and Background

In the 1920s and 1930s, skiing in California was shifting from a practical way of traveling in winter to a popular sport. The finishing of the transcontinental railroad and, later, upgrades to the highway over Donner Pass put the Sierra within reach of Bay Area residents. Groups of enthusiasts began forming clubs so they could share resources, organize trips, and build or rent lodges close to the slopes.

The Oakland Ski Club belonged to this movement. Like several of its contemporaries, such as the Sierra Club's lodges and other Bay Area ski clubs, it built a member-owned lodge near Donner Summit, which was then one of the main centers of skiing in Northern California thanks to its heavy snowfall and its rail and road access. The Donner Summit area also had some of the earliest ski lifts and ski schools in the western United States.

## How the Club Works

The club runs on a cooperative, member-based model, which is typical of traditional ski clubs:

- **Membership:** Members pay dues and often a one-time initiation fee. In return, they can use the lodge at reduced rates.
- **Lodge:** The lodge offers dormitory-style or shared rooms, a communal kitchen and dining area, and shared spaces such as a fireplace lounge. It lets members stay near resorts like Sugar Bowl, Donner Ski Ranch, Boreal and Soda Springs.
- **Work parties:** Members commonly give volunteer labor, such as cleaning, repairs, and getting the lodge ready for winter, which keeps costs down.
- **Guests:** Many clubs of this kind let members bring guests, and some rent space to the public or to groups when it is available.

## Activities

Although skiing and snowboarding are the main focus in winter, clubs like the Oakland Ski Club often arrange:

- Group ski trips and carpools from the Bay Area
- Social events, dinners and holiday gatherings
- Cross-country skiing and snowshoeing outings
- Summer activities such as hiking, mountain biking and lake trips, since the lodge can be used all year

## Significance

The Oakland Ski Club illustrates how ordinary Bay Area residents helped open up skiing before large commercial resorts arrived. Member-owned lodges made a ski weekend cheaper and built close-knit communities, many of which have endured for generations, with the children and grandchildren of early members still taking part. Exchange Programmes: A Good Idea or Not?

Nowadays many schools and universities have exchange programmes. In this programmes, students travel to a different country for some months or a year and they study there. Some people think it is a wonderful chance, but others think it is too hard or too expensive. I believe that joining an exchange programme is a good idea, but students need to prepare well.

The first reason is that an exchange programme help students improve a foreign language. If you live in another country, you have to speak the language every day, in the shop, on the bus and at school. This is different from learning in a classroom, because you truly need it. My cousin went to Germany for six months and when she come back, she speak German very well.

The second reason is that students can learn about a new culture. They taste new food, see different festivals and meet people with other ideas. That makes them more open-minded. They can also make friends from all around the world and perhaps keep this friendship for many years.

On the other hand, there are some problems too. Exchange programmes can cost a lot, and not every family is able to pay for travel and living costs. Some students feel homesick and lonely, especially in the first weeks. Sometimes the school system is very different, so it is hard to follow the lessons.

To conclude, I think the advantages are more big than the disadvantages. An exchange programme is a special experience that students can not get at home.
