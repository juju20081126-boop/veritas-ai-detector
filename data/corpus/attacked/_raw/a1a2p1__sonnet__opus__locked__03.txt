The cleric is the textbook support character in tabletop role-playing games such as Dungeons & Dragons and in many MMOs. Parties recruit one because clerics handle tasks that few other classes can.

**Why parties want a cleric**

- **Healing.** During and after fights, clerics bring hit points back, so fighters and other front-line characters can last through longer battles.
- **Recovery from disaster.** They cure poison, disease and curses, and at high levels they can even raise fallen characters from the dead.
- **Buffs and protection.** Spells such as Bless or Shield of Faith help the whole team hit more often and get hit less.
- **Handling undead.** A cleric can turn or destroy undead monsters, which takes much of the danger out of some dungeons.
- **Flexibility.** Plenty of clerics wear sturdy armor and can fight in melee, so they don't have to stay hidden in the back row.

**What happens without one**

A party can certainly get by with no cleric, but it must adjust:

- Healing potions become the group's crutch, and they cost gold and eventually run out.
- Characters have to rest more frequently, which may let enemies regroup or cause a time-limited quest to expire.
- Other classes, like druids, bards or paladins, might take on healing duty part of the time.
- Characters die more often, and the death can be permanent when no one can cast revival magic.
- Players grow more careful, avoid risky encounters, and lean harder on stealth or diplomacy.

In most current games, a cleric isn't strictly necessary, but it acts as a safety net. Remove it and each mistake costs more, which some groups welcome as an extra challenge.

**The Wall at Okafor's**

Each Sunday morning, stories filled the brick wall next to Okafor's Laundromat.

No one could say precisely how it began. One week a lone index card was thumbtacked to the corkboard by the door, six lines about a cat who thought she was a lighthouse. By the next Sunday there were eleven. By the close of that summer the board had stretched along the entire side of the building, and people came from three neighborhoods just to read it.

A strip of plywood at the top carried the one rule, painted on: LEAVE A STORY, LEAVE A COMMENT.

Comments went on sticky notes under each card. Most were kind. *Loved the ending. Who is the woman in the blue coat? Please write more about the cat.*

Some were unkind, and that was where Mrs. Okafor came in.

She had owned the laundromat for thirty-one years. Each Sunday evening she went down the length of the wall carrying a coffee can, taking off the cruel notes, the ads, and the cards that were really just phone numbers. She pinned back anything the wind had shaken loose. When rain was in the forecast, she draped a plastic tarp over the board. She never wrote a word herself.

Theo had been leaving stories since April, always unsigned, always on green cards so he could spot them again. He was fifteen and had never told a soul that he wrote. He read every comment on his cards twice, first fast and then slowly.

In October, a new card showed up at the top of the wall, in Mrs. Okafor's careful block capitals.

MY KNEES ARE GETTING OLD AND THE WALL IS GETTING BIG. I NEED HELP KEEPING IT. IF YOU LOVE THIS PLACE, COME INSIDE AND ASK.

Within a day, sticky notes crowded beneath it. *We love you, Mrs. O! Thank you for everything. Someone should totally do this.*

No one came inside.

That Sunday, Theo stood in front of the card for a long while. Then he pulled a green index card from his pocket, wrote three words, and pinned it under hers.

*I'll do it.*

As he pushed the door open, the dryers were humming, and Mrs. Okafor was already holding out a second coffee can.

"How did you know it was me?" he asked.

"Who do you think," she said, "has been keeping the rain off your stories since April?"

**Indonesia Executes Eight Drug Convicts Despite International Appeals**

JAKARTA — Early Wednesday, Indonesia put eight death row prisoners to death, among them two Australians and a Brazilian, drawing harsh condemnation from foreign governments that had spent months begging for clemency.

Shortly after midnight, the prisoners were shot by firing squad on Nusa Kambangan, a high-security prison island off Java's southern coast. All eight had been convicted of drug trafficking.

Those killed included the Australians Andrew Chan and Myuran Sukumaran, ringleaders of the so-called "Bali Nine," a group arrested in 2005 for trying to smuggle heroin out of Indonesia. The Brazilian Rodrigo Gularte, arrested in 2004 for trying to bring cocaine into the country hidden in surfboards, was executed too, as were four Nigerian men and one Indonesian.

A ninth prisoner, Mary Jane Veloso of the Philippines, was granted a last-minute reprieve after a woman suspected of recruiting her as a drug courier turned herself in to police in the Philippines. Veloso's supporters had argued that she was an unwitting victim of human trafficking.

**Brazil expresses 'deepest sympathy'**

Brazil's government said the news had arrived "with deep consternation" and offered its "deepest sympathy" to Gularte's family. Brazilian officials had asked Indonesia again and again to spare Gularte, contending that he had been diagnosed with schizophrenia and shouldn't be put to death because of his mental illness.

This was the second execution of a Brazilian in Indonesia this year. In January, Marco Archer Cardoso Moreira was executed for drug smuggling, and President Dilma Rousseff responded by recalling Brazil's ambassador from Jakarta. Ties between the countries have been strained ever since, and Rousseff had earlier put off accepting the credentials of Indonesia's new ambassador to Brazil.

**Australia condemns 'cruel and unnecessary' executions**

Australian Prime Minister Tony Abbott called the executions "cruel and unnecessary" and pointed out that Chan and Sukumaran had both been "fully rehabilitated" over their ten years in prison. Chan had become a pastor, and Sukumaran a skilled painter who gave art lessons to other inmates.

"We respect Indonesia's sovereignty, but we do deplore what's been done," Abbott told reporters in Canberra, announcing that Australia would withdraw its ambassador for consultations. Australia has never before recalled an ambassador from Indonesia over an execution.

In the days before the executions, the men's families had held a vigil on the island and made emotional public pleas to Indonesian President Joko Widodo.

**Widodo stands firm**

Widodo, who entered office in October, has turned down every clemency request in drug cases. He argues that Indonesia faces a narcotics "emergency" that kills dozens of young people daily. His government says foreign pressure won't sway its legal process, and officials have dismissed any suggestion that the executions had political motives.

The United Nations and human rights groups also criticized the executions. They argue that the death penalty doesn't effectively deter drug crime and that drug offenses fall short of the "most serious crimes" standard in international law. Amnesty International described the executions as "utterly reprehensible."

Indonesia restarted executions in 2013 after a four-year pause. Dozens more prisoners remain on death row for drug offenses, and officials have signaled that more executions are likely.
