A fractal's a shape that has detail at every magnification level. Usually it repeats itself, and the dimension doesn't have to be a whole number. In theory they're pure, but in the real world lots of physical systems look fractal-ish over some range of scales, which is why the idea's useful.

Nature has the best examples. Coastlines, river branches, mountains, clouds—all have irregular repeating patterns. The length or area you measure depends on how you measure it, which Benoit Mandelbrot figured out. Biology does similar stuff: bronchial tubes and blood vessels branch like crazy to pack huge surface area into small space, which lets them exchange stuff efficiently.

Fractals also show up in physics. When particles clump together at random, they branch like lightning. Chaotic systems often settle into fractal patterns. And near critical points where phase transitions happen, you get fractal scaling.

Practically, fractals help with antenna design, image compression, medical imaging, modeling porous materials and terrain.

But there are limits. Real objects are only fractal within some range—atoms at the small end, the object's size at the other. So fractal models are approximate, not literal. Still, they're really useful for quantifying messiness, which is why they're everywhere in science.

Polar-ring galaxies are rare. They have a ring of gas, dust, and stars orbiting nearly perpendicular to the central host's disk. Nobody's totally sure how they form—maybe a neighboring galaxy pulled the ring away, maybe cold gas fell in. We ran N-body simulations with gas dynamics and star formation to test whether a major galaxy merger could make one. We tried different mass ratios, orbital setups, and gas amounts. When the right conditions lined up—especially nearly perpendicular spin and unequal gas—the gas from one galaxy got stripped, settled into a perpendicular structure, and started forming stars. The ring hung around for billions of years and matched what we see in real polar-ring galaxies: the shape, rotation curves, stellar mass. The host had to be a relatively gas-poor early-type system. So mergers, not just accretion from a neighbor, can explain a lot of the polar-ring galaxies we observe.

Hard scattering angles are sensitive to gluon polarization in unpolarized hadrons. This polarization comes from perturbative effects, so it can create azimuthal modulations like cos 2φ in events with heavy quarks, dijets, and Higgs. The upper limit is set by positivity bounds, and people thought it could be pretty big. We looked at how soft gluon radiation affects this. When we resum large logarithms between the hard scale and pair transverse momentum, we found Sudakov suppression kills the polarized component much harder than the unpolarized one. So the asymmetries end up way below the maximum at kinematics where the factorized description works. We worked out the suppression for LHC kinematics and future electron-ion collider energies, showing how it depends on the hard scale and nonperturbative input. Earlier estimates were too optimistic. Something had thrown the clocks apart.

"Is that yours or mine?" Maya said.
