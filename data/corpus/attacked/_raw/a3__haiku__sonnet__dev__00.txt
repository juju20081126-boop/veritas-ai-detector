Should Students Take a Gap Year?

So a lot of people say you gotta go straight to college after high school, but honestly, a gap year can be pretty good if you actually plan it out.

First thing is, it actually helps you figure out what you want to do. I've got friends picking majors because it sounds fancy or because their parents told them to. But like, if you work for a year or volunteer or whatever, you get a better sense of what you actually care about. People who do that usually come back way more focused.

Plus you learn actual real-world stuff. You get a job, save money, maybe travel somewhere by yourself. That makes you independent in a way school doesn't teach you. Employers care about that.

But yeah, there are downsides too. It can cost a lot of money. And if you don't have a real plan, you might just get lazy and stop thinking about school. Then the whole year's kind of wasted.

So I think it works if you know what you want to do with it—make money, learn something specific, get some experience. If you already know what you wanna study, just go straight to university. Either way, just make sure you're actually choosing instead of just drifting.

Money vs. Happiness

Okay so people say money is like the key to happiness, and other people say no that's not true, happiness is something else. I think... yeah, money helps, but it's not the most important thing.

Ghosts

Ghosts take our stuff because they don't have anything else.

I learned that after my grandma died, when the house on Pell Street started eating my keys. Every single day in the same place—the blue bowl by the door. I'd put them down and a minute later, gone. At first I thought I was just losing it. Then my coffee mug disappeared. One earring. My reading glasses showed up a week later on the kitchen windowsill, folded neat, facing the street.

My brother Dev said I was going crazy. "Grief does that," he said. But then his sunglasses went missing and he stopped saying anything.

So I started leaving stuff out on purpose. A spoon. A button. A pencil. They'd always turn up somewhere else. The spoon on the stairs. The button on the piano. The pencil lined up next to my glasses on the sill.

One night I couldn't sleep. I sat in the dark kitchen and put down my grandma's thimble and just waited. Held my breath. The air got cold like before snow, and the thimble slid across the table, slow, a few inches at a time, until it reached the window edge where the streetlight came in.

That's when it clicked. My grandma used to sit at that window every evening, watching the street, waiting for people to come home. She was never good at saying she missed us. Just kept the porch light on and counted the cars.

The things weren't being stolen. She was collecting them. Each one was proof that the house was still full. A ghost doesn't have hands to hold a person. All it has is what people leave behind.

So I stopped trying to get my keys back. Next morning I put a cup of tea next to the thimble and said out loud, "I'm still here, Grandma."

The cup was empty by evening.

When I sold the house, I walked through with the new owners, young couple with a baby. I didn't warn them. Just said if they ever lost something small, don't worry too much. It'll show up on the windowsill.

Left the blue bowl by the door.

How to Actually Wake Up Feeling Good

Waking up rested is mostly about what you do the day before, not about your alarm.

Keep the same schedule. Bed and wake time should be roughly the same every day, even weekends. That's basically the most important thing. If you wake up two hours later on Saturday, you're basically jet-lagged on Monday.

Get enough sleep. Most people need seven to nine hours. Figure out when you need to be asleep based on when you wake up, and be honest about how long it actually takes you to fall asleep.

Wind down before bed. Dim lights an hour before, put your phone away—or at least stop notifications—and skip the heavy food, alcohol, and caffeine later in the day. Cool dark room helps a lot.

Let light in when you wake. Light is the biggest signal to your brain that it's morning. Open the curtains right away or go outside for a bit. If it's still dark when you wake, a bright lamp or one of those sunrise alarms really helps.

Don't snooze. That fragmented sleep after the first alarm is bad sleep and usually makes you groggier. Put your alarm across the room so you have to actually get up.

Move around and drink water. A glass of water and some stretching or a quick walk gets your blood going. Eating breakfast helps too, though it matters less than the rest.

Give yourself time. Changing habits takes a week or two to really feel. If you sleep enough and still feel exhausted every morning, talk to a doctor—could be sleep apnea or thyroid stuff.

The Scheveningen in the Sicilian

The Scheveningen (1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 e6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 d6) is a flexible setup for Black where you keep your pawns on d6 and e6 and don't commit your pieces right away. White has a bunch of good plans and it usually depends on what kind of player you are.

The English Attack (Be3, f3, Qd2, O-O-O, g4) is the hot setup right now and pretty scary. White castles queenside and goes after your king with g4-g5. You usually respond with a6, b5, and then Bb7 or Nbd7, looking for counterplay on the queenside and breaks like d5. It's sharp and you need to know theory.

The Classical setup (Be2, O-O, f4, then Be3, Qe1 or Qd2) is slower. White castles kingside and builds gradually, maybe f4 and a4, thinking about e5 or attacking your king. It's more strategic, pretty solid for White, but you've got real chances for counterplay.

Keres Attack is just 6.g4 immediately. Threatens to drive your f6 knight away with g5. Black usually plays h6 or brings a knight to c6, then tries for d5 or takes on d4 and plays e5.

The Sozin Attack (Bc4 and Be3) puts the bishop on c4 actively. White might castle kingside or queenside depending on how you set up. Can get sharp and you have to be careful about pressure on e6.

Fischer's plan is kind of like Sozin but with Qe2 and queenside castling. The point is you get crushed on the d-file if you're not careful.

Then there are the quiet setups—Be2, a4, Be3. White stops your b5 with a4 and plays slow chess, looking for small advantages on d6 and e6.

Big ideas across all these: White wants to use the space advantage, keep that d4 knight, look for tactics on e6, push e5, hit the weak squares. Black is going for d5 to equalize, b5 for queenside space, and counterattack if White castles queenside.

If you want to start playing this, English Attack is great if you like to attack. Classical if you prefer solid positional play.

The Roman Republic and Empire

Rome has this whole history that's kind of crazy.

So the story goes that Rome was founded in 753 BCE and had kings until 509 BCE when they kicked out Tarquin the Proud and set up a republic. Power was spread between consuls (two elected each year), the Senate (aristocrats who advised), and assemblies where people voted.

Early on there was this big conflict—plebeians (regular people) wanted political rights against the patricians (the rich guys). Took like two hundred years but they got to hold office, had tribunes to protect them, and in 450 BCE they got a written law code called the Twelve Tables.

Rome expanded by conquering the Italian peninsula and making deals with the people they beat. Then they fought Carthage three times—the Punic Wars, from 264 to 146 BCE. Hannibal invaded and almost destroyed Rome but they won and wiped out Carthage. Same period they took Greece, Spain, a bunch of the Mediterranean.

But conquest meant inequality and chaos. Small farmers lost their land. Generals got armies loyal to them instead of Rome. This guy Marius and Sulla fought each other, then Pompey and Caesar. Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE, won the civil war, and got stabbed. More fighting until Octavian beat Mark Antony and Cleopatra in 31 BCE.

Then the Empire started. Octavian became Augustus in 27 BCE, kept republican stuff but actually ruled. The Pax Romana started—couple centuries of relative peace and stability.

The empire got really big under Trajan (98-117 CE), stretched from Britain to Mesopotamia, maybe 50 to 70 million people. Cities, roads, trade everywhere.

Third century was rough—civil war, plague, invasions. Diocletian divided things east and west to manage it. Constantine legalized Christianity (which became official), and built Constantinople as the new capital.

The Western Empire got invaded by Goths, Vandals, other groups. The last emperor Romulus Augustulus got deposed in 476 CE. The Eastern half, the Byzantine Empire, lasted until 1453.

What's important: Roman law shaped legal systems everywhere—ideas like written laws, legal rights, presumption of innocence. The mixed government influenced modern thinkers and the US Constitution. They built roads, aqueducts, bridges, really good engineering. The legions were incredibly organized and effective. Latin turned into modern Romance languages. Writers like Cicero and Virgil are still read. Romans kept Greek philosophy and art alive. When Christianity became official it shaped medieval Europe.

Pentagon Says Coalition Has Taken Back Up to 30 Percent of Iraqi Territory From ISIS

The Pentagon released a map showing that coalition and Iraqi forces have retaken roughly 25 to 30 percent of the territory ISIS used to control in Iraq, according to defense officials.

The map shows areas that used to be held by the militants. It's meant to show how the campaign is going since airstrikes and ground operations ramped up. The numbers basically mean ISIS holds less land where people actually live.

But some analysts are saying don't get too excited about territory. Afzal Ashraf, who knows counterinsurgency, says you gotta look at this carefully.

"The map tells you something important, but not everything," Ashraf said. He pointed out that ISIS adapted—they're not standing in open formations anymore where air power can hit them.

"Where it counts, they're not fighting," Ashraf said. "They melt away, regroup, hit you when they choose."

So taking back territory doesn't necessarily mean you're destroying them. Militants leave one area and show up somewhere else. They do ambushes, IEDs, hit soft targets.

And holding territory once you take it is its own problem. Local security forces have to actually keep the militants out, and locals have to believe the government can protect them and provide services.

Defense officials say this is gonna be a long campaign and they're focused on training Iraqi forces. No timeline given.

Ashraf said people should look at other stuff too—whether ISIS has money, if they can recruit, if cleared areas are stable—to know if you're actually winning.
